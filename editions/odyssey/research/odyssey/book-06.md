# Book 6 — Nausicaa: how a stranger becomes a person

Primary: [Butler, Book 6](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.6.vi.html), Samuel Butler (1900), [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1727); quotations searchable in `sources/odyssey-06.txt`.

## Story [T]

Athena visits the princess Nausicaa in a dream, suggesting a washing expedition in preparation for marriage. Nausicaa asks her father for a wagon, explaining the family's need for clean clothing while leaving her own marriage unspoken. At the river the women wash, eat and play ball. Athena arranges the cry that wakes Odysseus. Naked and salt-encrusted, he emerges with a branch covering him. The attendants flee; Nausicaa stays, strengthened by Athena.

Odysseus decides to speak at a distance rather than clasp her knees. He praises her, describes his suffering and asks for clothing and directions. She offers assistance and calls the women back. He washes privately, puts on the clothes, and Athena enhances his appearance. Nausicaa wishes for a husband like him. She gives careful instructions for entering the city separately to avoid gossip, and tells him to appeal to her mother Arete. He waits in Athena's grove and prays.

## Quotations to mark

**B06-Q1 — Odysseus before meeting the women.**

> Are they cruel, savage, and uncivilised, or hospitable and humane?

Butler, Book 6, waking from the leaves. **[I]** The crucial test of the unfamiliar community is how it treats a vulnerable arrival. Butler's civilisational vocabulary should not be mistaken for a neutral modern anthropological classification.

**B06-Q2 — Narrator on Odysseus's chosen approach.**

> In the end he deemed it best to entreat her from a distance

Butler, Book 6, before his speech. **[I]** Practical intelligence includes understanding another person's possible fear. A customary gesture is not automatically appropriate in every situation.

**B06-Q3 — Odysseus's blessing on a future marriage.**

> for there is nothing better in this world than that man and wife should be of one mind in a house.

Butler, Book 6, close of the first speech. **[I]** The praise of shared-mindedness points towards his own reunion. It is also tactful speech addressed to a marriageable princess, so sincerity and persuasion need not be alternatives.

**B06-Q4 — Nausicaa addressing her attendants.**

> for strangers and foreigners in distress are under Jove’s protection,

Butler, Book 6, before washing and feeding him. **[I]** A stranger's claim to help precedes knowledge of his famous identity. Later recognition enhances his reception but is not the origin of this obligation.

## Important passages and close reading

**Essential: washing and public life. [I]** Clean garments serve council meetings, dances and anticipated marriage. Domestic work supports visible social standing. The expedition appears ordinary; divine intervention turns it into the hinge of the hero's rescue. This juxtaposition makes the apparently marginal work of women central to the continuation of the epic.

**Essential: the lion simile. [I]** The narrator compares Odysseus to a hungry lion approaching livestock. He is needy rather than intending to attack the women, yet the image makes their alarm intelligible. A sympathetic hero can be perceived as a threat by someone who lacks our information. We do not need to condemn the fleeing attendants to admire Nausicaa's response; the text explicitly gives her divine courage.

**Essential: verbal supplication at a distance. [I]** Odysseus chooses the form of his appeal before choosing its content. His speech moves from possible goddess to mortal family, from praise to distress, from bodily need to a blessing. He asks for a minimal covering rather than demanding an escort fit for a king. A person accustomed to command survives by respecting the recipient's position and making a request she can grant.

**[A] Counterreading:** his rhetorical skill can be admired without treating the speech as unmediated emotional transparency. Compliment, kinship language, religious comparison and marriage wishes are carefully adapted to the audience. The passage makes persuasion morally productive here, but it does not declare all persuasive language trustworthy.

**Essential: the change in appearance. [T/I]** Nausicaa helps before Odysseus becomes handsome. After washing, clothes and Athena's intervention, she desires someone like him. This sequence matters: need first elicits aid, appearance then changes the imagined relationship. A simplified romance would reverse the ethical order. The artisan simile for his enhancement also returns to craft after the raft-building in Book 5.

**Essential: Nausicaa's imagined gossip. [I]** She supplies a miniature speech by a hypothetical critic. She can manage the encounter but cannot easily determine the public meaning of being seen with a man. Her directions embody both agency and constraint. The poem lets her anticipate judgement in other people's language. She even says she would judge another woman similarly, showing how a social rule can be reproduced by someone limited by it.

**Do not skip: the pace of the wagon. [I]** She slows enough for the people walking. This is a small behavioural detail worth placing beside her grand declaration of hospitality. Care is a principle expressed through timing, distance, food and practical instructions.

## Myth and cultural context [T/C]

The narrator compares Nausicaa to Artemis among nymphs; Odysseus makes the same identification as a possibility in his praise. Nausicaa is not literally Artemis. He also recalls a palm at Apollo's altar on Delos. Divine comparison marks beauty and stature while allowing a respectful approach.

Supplication and hospitality overlap but are not identical. The first is an appeal from a vulnerable position for protection; the second governs reception and exchange between host and guest. He adapts the bodily gesture of supplication because of the circumstances. Nausicaa's Phaeacians are seafarers whose prior migration from the Cyclopes is narrated at the start. Their apparent isolation is therefore a historical claim inside the story, not a reason to call them an uncomplicated timeless paradise.

## Philosophical stakes and example [I/M]

The book makes recognition an ethical practice before it becomes identification. Nausicaa need not know “this is Odysseus” to know “this person needs food and clothing.” At the same time the two parties must negotiate safety and reputation. Hospitality is generous without being socially frictionless.

**[M] Example:** offering help to a visibly distressed stranger can involve maintaining respectful distance, speaking clearly and explaining a safe next step. The analogy illustrates the scene's attentiveness; it is not a general instruction to ignore danger.

## Joyce and questions [M]

Joyce's [Episode 13, “Nausicaa”](../ulysses/episode-13.md), reworks a shoreline encounter through looking, desire and competing fantasies. Gerty is not simply a modern princess who rescues Bloom; the differences in mutual knowledge and erotic perspective are central.

3. Where does Nausicaa act freely, and where must she manage a judgement she cannot control?
