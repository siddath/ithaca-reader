# Book 2 — Speech meets power; Penelope works on time

Primary reading: [Butler, Book 2](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.2.ii.html); exact quotation source `sources/odyssey-02.txt`, Samuel Butler (1900), [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1727). Quotations are searchable phrase locators, not Greek verse numbers.

## What happens [T]

Telemachus convenes the first assembly since Odysseus sailed away. The elder Aegyptius hopes it will address an important public matter. Telemachus describes his household's destruction. Antinous counters by blaming Penelope: she promised to choose a husband after completing Laertes' burial cloth, but secretly undid the day's work each night until a woman in her household disclosed the trick. He demands that Telemachus send her away to marry.

Telemachus refuses, invoking practical costs, his mother's claim upon him and the Erinyes. Two eagles appear; Halitherses interprets them as a warning of Odysseus's return. Eurymachus rejects the warning and threatens its interpreter. Mentor rebukes the silent community. The assembly dissolves without protecting the household. Telemachus prays; Athena, now taking Mentor's appearance, assists him. Euryclea prepares supplies under an oath of secrecy. Athena recruits a crew in Telemachus's likeness, obtains a ship and returns as Mentor. They depart at night.

## Quotations to mark

**B02-Q1 — Telemachus on the public consequences of private consumption.**

> No estate can stand such recklessness;

Butler, Book 2, assembly speech. **[I]** The hero's adventure begins with accounting: food, livestock and wine are finite. The material crisis gives urgency to apparently ceremonial questions of marriage and rank.

**B02-Q2 — Antinous reporting Penelope's nightly action.**

> but at night she would unpick the stitches again by torchlight.

Butler, Book 2, Antinous's account of the shroud. **[T/I]** The first major report of her cunning comes from an adversary. His grievance supplies evidence of her resourcefulness despite his attempt to condemn it.

**B02-Q3 — Eurymachus dismissing the omen.**

> birds are always flying about in the sunshine somewhere or other, but they seldom mean anything.

Butler, Book 2, reply to Halitherses. **[I]** A plausible general observation becomes a convenient way to reject an inconvenient particular warning. His subsequent threat matters as much as his scepticism.

**B02-Q4 — Mentor addressing the assembly.**

> for you are many and they are few.

Butler, Book 2, rebuke to the Ithacans. **[I]** The scene extends responsibility beyond the people doing direct harm to those who could obstruct it and remain passive.

## Important passages

**Essential: Telemachus's tears and the dropped staff. [I/A]** Public authority does not arrive as flawless performance. He cannot yet compel what he requests; emotion breaks through his formal speech. Calling him merely weak misses the action he has already taken. Calling the assembly a victory misses its practical failure. The poem lets partial development be visible without granting immediate success.

**Essential: the shroud story. [I]** Penelope uses an approved household skill and an obligation to an older man to delay the marriage expected of her. The cloth is intended for Laertes, who is still alive, not for Odysseus. This distinction matters: she presents the work as preparation for a legitimate future duty while undoing another future demanded by the suitors. Weaving creates; unweaving preserves an opening. This is the strongest local basis for reading weaving as a figure for controlling narrative and time. It does not prove that every cloth anywhere in Greek myth means the same thing.

**[A] Limit:** the trick is temporary and depends on secrecy and the labour setting of an unequal household. Antinous's account reveals it after its exposure. Penelope's agency is neither absent nor unlimited. Revisit her own version in Book 19 and the suitor's version in Book 24: who gets to tell the story changes its meaning.

**Essential: disagreement about the birds. [T/I]** Halitherses interprets; Eurymachus dismisses. The narrator explicitly identifies Zeus as sender, so the audience's privileged information differs from the uncertainty of an ordinary observer. The scene should not be used to argue that real-world bird movements supply reliable predictions. Within the poem, refusing the omen belongs to a wider refusal to heed restraint; in practical human inquiry, scepticism can be justified. Keep those frames separate.

**Essential: the assembly's dissolution. [I]** Telemachus describes his grievance as personal, yet it implicates the community's leading families, standards of hospitality, succession and collective enforcement. The distinction between public and private breaks down in practice. Mentor's rebuke asks whether good rule creates a reciprocal obligation in the ruled. Nothing here requires importing modern democracy: this is an assembly within the poem's elite political world, not an Athenian institution transplanted backwards.

**Do not skip: the stores and Euryclea's oath. [I]** Preserved wine and carefully packed grain convert longing into logistics. Euryclea anticipates the risk of a plot against the son, which Book 4 confirms. Concealment from Penelope protects the departure but also excludes her from a decision that deeply affects her. The poem's coming-of-age story includes dependence on an enslaved woman's knowledge and work.

## Myth and social context [T/C]

Themis is invoked with Zeus in the assembly; the Erinyes enter Telemachus's fear of wronging his mother. Divine order therefore appears through several relationships, not simply obedience to one Olympian. The text distinguishes the human Mentor who speaks in the assembly from Athena appearing as Mentor afterwards. Athena also takes Telemachus's shape to recruit the crew. Conflating all three appearances makes the human community's failure disappear.

For this distinction see [Stamatia Dova on mentors and kings](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/stamatia-dova-kind-like-a-father-on-mentors-and-kings-in-the-odyssey/). Our account follows the primary scene; the scholar is a useful further investigation of the changing Mentor role.

## Philosophical and metaphorical analysis [I]

**Power is more than having the better argument.** Telemachus can articulate an injustice and still lack the means to remedy it. The gap between recognition and enforcement gives the coming violent restoration some of its narrative preparation without settling whether every eventual act is just.

**Delay can be an action.** Penelope's refusal to finish is purposeful maintenance of possibility. But celebration of delay must include its cost: continued consumption of the estate and prolonged uncertainty. One strategy can both resist coercion and deepen another vulnerability.

**Learning does not mean independence from help.** Athena does much of the organising. Telemachus nevertheless makes requests, assembles provisions and boards the ship. The opposition between total self-reliance and passivity is too crude for this scene.

**[M] Example:** a person facing an unfair institutional process may use its own procedural requirements to gain time. That resembles the structure of the shroud strategy, but Penelope's position is specifically shaped by marriage, kinship and household power.
