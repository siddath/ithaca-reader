# Book IX — Nobody, the Guest-Gift, and the Cost of a Name

Primary text: [Samuel Butler, *Odyssey* IX](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.9.ix.html). Local reading text: `sources/odyssey-09.txt`. Butler uses the Roman names Jove, Minerva, and Neptune for Zeus, Athena, and Poseidon; this dossier uses his wording only in quotations.

## What happens, and who knows what

[T] At Alcinous' feast Odysseus finally names himself and becomes the narrator of Books IX–XII. He first recalls the raid on the Cicones: his force sacks Ismarus, kills men, enslaves women, and takes booty. His men then refuse his order to depart; a Ciconian counterattack kills six men from every ship. That opening matters because the wanderings do not begin with innocent victims encountering monsters. Odysseus' own story begins with Greek aggression and captive-taking.

> I am Ulysses son of Laertes, renowned among mankind for all manner of subtlety, so that my fame ascends to heaven.

Odysseus to the Phaeacians; Book IX, searchable phrase “I am Ulysses son of Laertes”; local lines 19–23.

[T] Storms drive the fleet to the Lotus-eaters. Three scouts accept food and lose the wish for return; Odysseus forces them aboard. At the Cyclopes' land he chooses twelve companions and enters Polyphemus' cave. His crew proposes taking cheese and animals and leaving. Odysseus stays because he expects a guest-gift. Polyphemus returns, seals the cave, rejects Zeus' protection of strangers, and eats six men over two nights. Odysseus intoxicates him with Maron's wine, calls himself “Noman,” blinds him, and escapes by tying the men beneath rams. The other Cyclopes misunderstand Polyphemus' cry that “Noman” hurts him. Once safe, Odysseus boasts his true name; Polyphemus invokes Poseidon, his father, to delay or ruin the homecoming.

> It would have been indeed better if we had done so but I would not listen to them, for I wanted to see the owner himself, in the hope that he might give me a present.

Odysseus about his crew's advice; Book IX, searchable phrase “It would have been indeed better”; local lines 188–194.

> my name is Noman; this is what my father and mother and my friends have always called me.

Odysseus to Polyphemus; Book IX, searchable phrase “my name is Noman”; local lines 317–319.

> though they wept bitterly I forced them back to the ships and made them fast under the benches.

Odysseus about the lotus-eaters; Book IX, searchable phrase “made them fast under the benches”; local lines 81–88.

## The passages to hold onto

[T] The most important sequence is the cave from the crew's prudent proposal through the final boast (local lines 181–474). It stages hospitality, intelligence, violence, and fame as one connected test. The “Noman” device works because language reorganizes social knowledge: Odysseus' comrades know his identity, Polyphemus knows a false identity, and the neighboring Cyclopes hear a grammatically plausible denial of an attacker. The boast reverses that achievement by giving the victim the information needed to call down Poseidon's curse.

[C] *Xenia* is reciprocal guest-friendship under Zeus: a host shelters and feeds before demanding identity, while a guest respects the house and may receive a gift. Polyphemus grotesquely reverses the meal and gift by eating guests and promising to eat “Noman” last. Yet Odysseus also enters an absent owner's home, consumes food, and assumes a gift is owed. The scene tests both sides of the institution rather than making Greek conduct automatically just.

[C] The Cyclops existed in wider Greek myth, and later art and literature elaborate one-eyed giants. This dossier keeps to Homer's Polyphemus unless later tradition is named. Maron's wine comes from the earlier sack, but Odysseus says he spared Maron, Apollo's priest, and family; a reciprocal gift produced by one restraint becomes the tool that saves survivors.

## Close readings

[I] The chapter's deepest conflict is between *nostos* (return) and *kleos* (fame). The lotus erases the desire for return; Polyphemus threatens the body that must return; Odysseus' boast endangers return for the sake of a named story. Gregory Nagy's formulation that Odysseus' distinctive fame depends on achieving and narrating homecoming clarifies the paradox: “Noman” saves the homecomer, but “Odysseus” creates the tale. [A] The boast can be read as reckless vanity. It can also be read as an attempt to make punishment socially legible: anonymous violence cannot establish who answered the violation of hospitality. The poem makes that demand for recognition catastrophically expensive.

[I] The Cyclopes' lack of assemblies, agriculture, shipbuilding, and mutual concern defines them through an outsider's civilizational checklist. The lush unused island invites a colonial gaze: Odysseus imagines how sailors “would have colonised” it. [A] Readers should resist treating this description as neutral anthropology. It is Odysseus' interested narration to a seafaring court, and Polyphemus' careful dairying complicates the label of pure disorder.

[I] Authority over the lotus-eaters is morally double. Odysseus preserves his companions' long-term project by overriding their altered wishes and physically binding them. [A] That can represent necessary command under intoxication; it also reveals how the leader defines the group's true desire and uses force to enforce it. The poem does not let leadership remain clean.

[I] Power and victimhood change places without canceling each other. The Cicones' wives are treated as divisible booty, while Polyphemus later treats Greek bodies as food. The parallel does not equate the acts; it prevents a reading in which only non-Greeks violate persons.

## Motifs, links, and modern comparison

[T] Recurrent links: appetite that defeats return anticipates Circe and the cattle of the Sun; binding here anticipates the mast in XII; the ram escape converts an animal household into concealment; Poseidon's curse governs the sea journey through XIII; recognition by names and signs returns in XIX, XXIII, and XXIV.

[M] Modern analogy: “Noman” resembles an operational alias that protects a rescue team, while the later boast resembles posting identifying details after the operation. The analogy explains the information failure, but the heroic culture adds something modern security language lacks: public credit is itself a form of survival.

## Questions

1. [I] Does Odysseus enter the cave primarily as a guest, a raider, or a storyteller seeking an encounter?
2. [A] When does command become coercion in the lotus episode, and what evidence would distinguish rescue from domination?
3. [I] Can Odysseus secure both homecoming and fame, or does Book IX show that each repeatedly endangers the other?

## Provisional Joyce bridge

[M] Joyce's “Cyclops” episode concentrates on aggressive nationalism, one-eyed certainty, hospitality, and the politics of naming. The useful parallel is formal rather than one-to-one: a closed social space turns identity claims into weapons, and verbal ingenuity both escapes and inflames violence. This is a reading prompt, not proof of direct equivalence for every scene.
