# Book XXIV — After the Return: Memory, Laertes, and an Imposed Peace

Primary text: [Samuel Butler, *Odyssey* XXIV](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.24.xxiv.html). Local text: `sources/odyssey-24.txt`. Butler's Minerva and Jove are Athena and Zeus.

## What happens, and who knows what

[T] Hermes conducts the suitors' shades to Hades, where Achilles and Agamemnon compare their deaths. Agamemnon recounts Achilles' magnificent funeral and enduring fame. Amphimedon then tells the suitors' story from a dead suitor's perspective: he describes Penelope's web, claims that she devised the bow contest after Odysseus returned, and emphasizes their unburied bodies. Agamemnon answers by praising Penelope's enduring fame and contrasting Clytemnestra.

> Thus even in death your fame, Achilles, has not been lost, and your name lives evermore among all mankind.

Agamemnon to Achilles; Book XXIV, searchable phrase “your fame, Achilles”; local lines 75–87.

> she meant to compass our destruction

Amphimedon's claim about Penelope; Book XXIV, searchable phrase “compass our destruction”; local lines 107–113. This is interested testimony, not settled proof that Penelope coordinated the contest with Odysseus.

> The fame, therefore, of her virtue shall never die

Agamemnon about Penelope; Book XXIV, searchable phrase “fame, therefore”; local lines 161–168. The surrounding generalization against women is Agamemnon's speech, shaped by his murder.

[T] Odysseus reaches Laertes' orchard but chooses another test before revealing himself. He praises the farm, tells a false story of hosting Odysseus, and watches Laertes collapse in grief. He then embraces him, shows the scar, and names the trees Laertes gave him as a child. Laertes accepts these proofs. Athena restores the old man's vigor; three generations and loyal dependents eat together.

> You gave me thirteen pear trees, ten apple trees, and forty fig trees

Odysseus to Laertes; Book XXIV, searchable phrase “thirteen pear trees”; local lines 274–284.

[T] News reaches the suitors' families. At an Ithacan assembly Eupeithes demands revenge; Medon attributes the event to divine support, and Halitherses blames the community for failing to restrain the suitors. Some withdraw, others march. Zeus proposes a covenant under which Odysseus continues ruling and the families are made to forget or relinquish vengeance. Laertes kills Eupeithes. Athena orders cessation; Odysseus charges again until Zeus' thunderbolt and Athena's explicit threat stop him. Athena, as Mentor, establishes peace.

> stop this warful strife, or Jove will be angry with you.

Athena to Odysseus; Book XXIV, searchable phrase “stop this warful strife”; local lines 457–461.

## The passages to hold onto

[T] The second underworld (local lines 1–169) is a contested retrospective, not neutral summary. Agamemnon and Amphimedon interpret events through their deaths. The suitors' lack of burial is real within the report; Amphimedon's claim about Penelope's coordination exceeds what earlier books explicitly establish.

[T] The orchard recognition (173–304) pairs scar with cultivated memory. The enumerated trees are not arbitrary passwords: they recall a father teaching a child names, gifts, and land. Recognition restores Odysseus as son after husband, father, king, and avenger.

[T] The final assembly and battle (341–465) reveal the public cost suppressed by the wedding music in XXIII. The dead have kin, the community is divided, and revenge generates a counterclaim. Peace arrives through divine command after another death, not through a fully narrated civic agreement.

[C] Ancient scholia report that Aristophanes of Byzantium and Aristarchus treated Odyssey 23.296 as a *telos/peras*. Stephanie West stresses that the evidence is disputed: it may concern textual authenticity of the continuation or aesthetic completion, and it should not be stated simply as “ancient scholars rejected Book XXIV.” Irene de Jong argues that XXIV supplies ring composition, closes Trojan veteran comparisons, returns major characters, and addresses consequences.

## Close readings

[I] Book XXIV asks who owns the ending. Agamemnon turns Penelope into exemplary song; Amphimedon turns the suitors into deceived victims; Laertes receives a son's autobiographical proof; the relatives frame the killings as massacre; Athena and Zeus impose the political settlement. No single retrospective exhausts the event.

[I] Odysseus tests Laertes even after seeing aged grief. [A] Murnaghan reads the recognition sequence as part of the removal of disguise and recovery of proper role, with orchard, bath, and bed motifs. A counterreading asks why proof requires intensifying a vulnerable father's sorrow when direct scar-and-tree evidence was available.

[I] The trees turn identity into ecological memory. The bed's olive anchors marriage; the orchard anchors intergenerational belonging and cultivated justice. The “cultivation” image supports Nagy's justice theme because rule is imagined as maintained growth, not victory alone. [A] Land gifts also encode elite inheritance and ownership; cultivation should not be romanticized apart from household labor.

[I] The ending does not show Odysseus voluntarily transcending violence. He obeys Athena “gladly” only after he has ignored the first command to cease, charged like an eagle, and been stopped by Zeus' thunderbolt and a threat. Peace is real in narrative outcome but externally enforced.

[I] Zeus' proposal that relatives “forgive and forget” stabilizes rule after the poem has shown the families retrieve and bury their dead, mourn, and assemble. It provides no compensation or fully deliberated consent to the covenant. [A] Within epic closure, divine forgetting can end feud and permit prosperity. A political counterreading sees unresolved injury suppressed to preserve the restored king.

[I] Agamemnon's praise grants Penelope *kleos*, but his contrast with Clytemnestra generalizes from two elite wives to female reputation. The poem gives him the speech; it need not require readers to adopt his misogynistic inference.

## Motifs, links, and modern comparison

[T] Links: Hades revisits XI; Achilles and Agamemnon complete the homecoming/death comparison; the shroud returns from XIX; scar and trees complete the recognition series; assembly echoes II; eagle imagery moves from omens and grief to Odysseus' final charge; divine councils frame the poem.

[M] Modern analogy: regime restoration succeeds militarily, then meets claims from the families of those killed. A higher authority freezes retaliation and recognizes the ruler without a truth process. That may stop immediate bloodshed while leaving reconciliation institutionally thin.

## Questions

1. [A] Does divine forgetting create peace, suspend conflict, or erase legitimate grievance?
2. [I] Why does Odysseus test Laertes, and what does the orchard prove that the scar alone cannot?
3. [U] How should the disputed ancient end-point at 23.296 affect our reading of XXIV's authority and necessity?

## Provisional Joyce bridge

[M] No forced “24:18” mapping is useful. Joyce closes with Molly's unpunctuated interior affirmation rather than a divine armistice among armed kin. The contrast asks what modernity does to epic closure: public feud and restored kingship give way to private, bodily, linguistically open continuation.
