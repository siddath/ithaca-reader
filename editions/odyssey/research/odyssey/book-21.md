# Book XXI — The Bow: Inheritance, Exclusion, and the Sound Before Violence

Primary text: [Samuel Butler, *Odyssey* XXI](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.21.xxi.html). Local text: `sources/odyssey-21.txt`. Butler's Minerva, Jove, and Apollo are Athena, Zeus, and Apollo.

## What happens, and who knows what

[T] Athena prompts Penelope to bring out Odysseus' bow and twelve axe-heads and propose marriage to whoever can string it and shoot through them. The bow has a history: Iphitus gave it to Odysseus in guest-friendship; Heracles later murdered Iphitus while hosting him and kept his horses. Penelope weeps over the object before presenting the trial.

> whomsoever of you shall string it most easily and send his arrow through each one of twelve axes, him will I follow and quit this house

Penelope to the suitors; Book XXI, searchable phrase “twelve axes”; local lines 59–66.

[T] Telemachus aligns the axes and nearly strings the bow on his fourth attempt, but Odysseus signals him to stop. The suitors fail in turn. Outside, Odysseus reveals himself to Eumaeus and Philoetius, using the scar as proof, and assigns them the doors and bow. Back inside he requests a trial. Penelope supports his right as a guest; Telemachus asserts control and sends her upstairs. Eumaeus delivers the bow despite threats, Euryclea closes the women's quarters, and Philoetius locks the outer gate.

> people who persist in eating up the estate of a great chieftain and dishonouring his house must not expect others to think well of them.

Penelope to Eurymachus; Book XXI, searchable phrase “must not expect”; local lines 288–290.

[T] Odysseus examines and strings the bow as easily as a musician tunes a lyre. The string sings like a swallow; Zeus thunders. Still seated, he sends the arrow cleanly through the axes and signals Telemachus, who arms beside him.

> it sang sweetly under his touch like the twittering of a swallow.

Narrator about the bowstring; Book XXI, searchable phrase “twittering of a swallow”; local lines 354–360.

> I did not miss what I aimed at, and I was not long in stringing my bow.

Odysseus to Telemachus; Book XXI, searchable phrase “did not miss”; local lines 369–373.

## The passages to hold onto

[T] The bow's origin (local lines 8–38) makes it more than a strength test. It is a surviving guest-gift from a friendship cut short by Heracles' violation of hospitality. That history quietly judges the suitors before the contest starts: a weapon received through reciprocity returns against men who have corrupted it.

[T] The delivery and locking sequence (299–343) is operationally decisive. Penelope is removed, women are confined behind doors, the exterior exit is secured, and the weapon reaches Odysseus. The public “contest” becomes a sealed kill-space without most participants knowing the transition.

[C] Bow use in epic is technically demanding and can carry different heroic associations from close spear combat. The poem makes Odysseus' competence artisanal and musical, not brute force alone. [U] The physical arrangement of the axe-head shot is debated; Butler's prose says “handle-holes,” but readers should not assume a single archaeological reconstruction.

## Close readings

[I] Penelope authors the immediate contest, though Athena places the idea in her mind. The text does not state that she and Odysseus privately coordinated it. Her choice creates the opening he uses, but shared intelligence need not imply shared information.

[I] Telemachus' near-success marks succession and restraint. He can almost perform the father's feat, then obeys a sign to stop. [A] This may show emerging equivalence without rivalry; it may also show the father preserving sole control of recognition and violence.

[I] The bow converts identity into observable skill. A scar can be read by intimates; the bow can be witnessed by enemies. Yet the suitors interpret the beggar's handling through class prejudice and cannot update until the demonstration is complete.

[I] Penelope defends the stranger, then Telemachus sends her to the loom and declares the bow a man's matter. The tactic may protect her from imminent violence, but his language also claims household authority by restricting hers. [A] Protective effect and patriarchal assertion can coexist.

[I] Eumaeus and Philoetius are embraced and promised wives, property, and brother-like status. Their recognition recruits indispensable allies, as Murnaghan emphasizes. The promise remains hierarchical: the returning owner allocates land, marriage prospects, and kin-like standing to dependent men.

[I] Sweet song immediately precedes slaughter. The lyre simile aestheticizes flawless control without making the coming deaths bloodless. The same culture that crowns banquets with music turns the bow into an instrument whose “song” closes hospitality.

## Motifs, links, and modern comparison

[T] Links: Iphitus' violated hospitality echoes Polyphemus and the suitors; scar proof repeats XIX; closed doors fulfill weapon-removal planning from XVI/XIX; craft links Odysseus to Penelope's weaving and the bed he built; Apollo's festival shadows the killings.

[M] Modern analogy: the contest resembles a familiar system used as an authentication challenge. Everyone can possess the interface, but only the original operator knows its full handling. Meanwhile access controls are quietly changed around the users—a technically brilliant maneuver whose ethical evaluation depends on what happens next.

## Questions

1. [I] What does the bow's history with Iphitus add to the suitors' trial?
2. [A] Does Telemachus' order protect Penelope, diminish her authority, or both?
3. [U] How much does Penelope know about the consequences of the contest she announces?

## Provisional Joyce bridge

[M] Joyce does not preserve a simple bow-test episode. The strongest bridge is formal: an ordinary object becomes an identity-bearing technology whose correct performance reorganizes every relationship in the room.
