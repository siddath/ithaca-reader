# Book XXII — The Reckoning and Its Excesses

Primary text: [Samuel Butler, *Odyssey* XXII](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.22.xxii.html). Local text: `sources/odyssey-22.txt`. Butler's Minerva and Jove are Athena and Zeus.

## What happens, and who knows what

[T] Odysseus shoots Antinous without warning as he raises a cup. The suitors first think it accidental. Odysseus reveals himself and accuses them of consuming his estate, courting his wife, and forcing women servants to have sex. Eurymachus places primary blame on the dead Antinous and offers collective restitution. Odysseus refuses every settlement and promises no quarter. Fighting begins in the locked hall.

> have forced my women servants to lie with you, and have wooed my wife while I was still living.

Odysseus accusing the suitors; Book XXII, searchable phrase “forced my women servants”; local lines 36–39. The excerpt begins after Butler's footnote marker. This is Odysseus' claim and is essential evidence against treating all servant-suitor sex as freely chosen.

> Each one of us shall pay you a fine worth twenty oxen, and we will keep on giving you gold and bronze till your heart is softened.

Eurymachus proposing restitution; Book XXII, searchable phrase “twenty oxen”; local lines 45–56.

[T] Telemachus arms Odysseus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius but leaves the store-room open; Melanthius supplies armor to the suitors until the two herdsmen capture and bind him. Athena appears as Mentor, tests Odysseus, then deflects spears and displays the aegis. The four kill the trapped suitors. Leiodes pleads that he opposed their wrongdoing; Odysseus infers guilt from his priestly role and beheads him while he speaks. Phemius says he sang under compulsion; Telemachus confirms this and saves him, along with Medon.

> the man is guiltless, do him no hurt; and we will spare Medon too

Telemachus about Phemius and Medon; Book XXII, searchable phrase “man is guiltless”; local lines 301–305.

[T] Euryclea identifies twelve of fifty women as disloyal. Odysseus orders them to carry bodies and clean blood, then tells Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius to kill them with swords. Telemachus substitutes hanging because he refuses them a “clean death.” Their feet convulse. Melanthius is mutilated and dismembered. Odysseus burns sulphur to purify the house, and the remaining women embrace him.

> even so did the women have to put their heads in nooses one after the other and die most miserably.

Narrator; Book XXII, searchable phrase “die most miserably”; local lines 392–399.

> There they cut off his nose and his ears; they drew out his vitals and gave them to the dogs raw

## The passages to hold onto

[T] Read the failed negotiation (local lines 36–79) without assuming either speaker's framing is complete. Eurymachus may opportunistically scapegoat Antinous, but he offers measurable restitution. Odysseus explicitly rejects compensation and escape before the surviving suitors arm themselves.

[T] Read the pleas of Leiodes and Phemius together (264–305). Leiodes' earlier Book XXI description says he hated the suitors' deeds; Odysseus interprets his priesthood as proof of hostile prayers and kills him. Phemius' coercion is independently confirmed by Telemachus, producing mercy. The contrast shows judgment occurring rapidly under rage, with uneven evidence.

[T] The executions at lines 350–420 must not be sanitized as purification. The women are forced to clean the aftermath, hanged collectively, and compared to trapped birds; Melanthius is tortured and dismembered. Sulphur follows physical killing but cannot itself settle justice.

[C] The household is a political-economic unit, and the suitors' acts threaten succession, property, marriage, and rule. Feud and retaliation remain live in XXIV. These social structures explain the stakes; they do not make every punishment proportionate by definition.

## Close readings

[I] The book moves from targeted vengeance to totalized extermination. Antinous has led abuses and the Telemachus plot; once he is dead, Odysseus refuses restitution from all. [A] Epic justice can read collective participation in the occupation as collective guilt. A counterreading asks whether locked-room killing after rejecting surrender exceeds restoration and renews the cycle of blood debt.

[I] Athena's aid is causal, not a symbol for confidence. She tests, protects, terrifies, and helps decide the battle. Human planning remains necessary, but divine support makes the force radically unequal.

[I] The twelve women sit at the intersection of enslavement and sexual coercion. Euryclea accuses them of disrespect and Telemachus says they slept with the suitors; Odysseus has just said women were forced. [U] The text does not supply each woman's history or establish that all sexual contact was freely chosen. Execution erases distinctions the narrative itself raises.

[I] Telemachus' mercy toward Phemius and Medon shows that complicity can be evaluated through coercion and prior conduct. His chosen hanging of the women shows the opposite: he intensifies punishment and speaks of sexual behavior as dishonor. The young ruler is not moving along a simple moral ascent.

[I] Euryclea's desire to exult is checked by Odysseus, who says it is wrong to rejoice over the dead. That ethical sentence coexists with mutilation and mass killing. [A] It may mark humility before divine judgment, or an unstable self-image that distinguishes solemn vengeance from others' pleasure while preserving his own violence.

## Motifs, links, and modern comparison

[T] Links: the feast becomes slaughter; bird similes reverse the raptor imagery of XV–XVI; forced song recalls Demodocus but under coercion; purification recalls ritual boundaries yet cannot stop kin retaliation; XXIV reopens the public consequences.

[M] Modern analogy: a compromised organization is retaken by its owner, who identifies genuine fraud but rejects surrender, compensation, and individualized hearings. The analogy clarifies why “restoring control” and “administering justice” are separate claims requiring separate proof.

## Questions

1. [A] At what point, if any, does justified defense become excessive punishment?
2. [I] Why is coercion credited in Phemius' case but not investigated for the women?
3. [I] What can sulphur purification accomplish after violence, and what can it not accomplish?

## Provisional Joyce bridge

[M] Joyce refuses to reproduce a climactic massacre. The absence matters: modern homecoming is not secured by heroic extermination. Book XXII can therefore serve as a pressure point for asking what Joyce retains from the return plot once sovereign household violence is displaced.
