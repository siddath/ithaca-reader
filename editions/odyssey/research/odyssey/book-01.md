# Book 1 — A missing man, a troubled house, and the beginning of action

Read: [Butler's Book 1 at MIT](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.1.i.html). Quotation baseline: Samuel Butler, *The Odyssey* (1900), [Project Gutenberg 1727](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1727); local `sources/odyssey-01.txt`. Search each quoted phrase in that file. Greek names in the commentary: Odysseus = Butler's Ulysses; Athena = Minerva; Zeus = Jove; Poseidon = Neptune; Hermes = Mercury. Labels follow [the contract](../../CONTRACT.md).

## Story and the distribution of knowledge [T]

The narrator asks the Muse to tell of a resourceful traveller whose men have died. We learn the outcome before the adventures: their eating the Sun god's cattle destroyed them. Odysseus survives, detained by Calypso, while Poseidon opposes his return. At a divine council, Zeus reflects on Aegisthus, who ignored a warning and was killed by Orestes after murdering Orestes' father Agamemnon. Athena redirects attention to Odysseus. She proposes a coordinated rescue and travels to Ithaca disguised as Mentes.

Odysseus's household is occupied by aristocratic men courting Penelope and consuming the estate. His son Telemachus receives the stranger courteously. Athena encourages him to convene an assembly and visit Pylos and Sparta for news. When she departs, he understands that a god has visited him. Penelope asks the singer Phemius to stop singing painful homecoming stories; Telemachus sends her back to her work and asserts authority. He announces a public confrontation for the next day. The book ends with the nurse Euryclea preparing his room and the young man thinking about his journey.

The reader knows Odysseus is alive; Telemachus does not know this with the narrator's certainty. Penelope's uncertainty is not foolishness. Dramatic irony makes waiting, rather than discovering whether the hero exists, the first emotional problem.

## Quotations to mark

**B01-Q1 — The narrator's opening description; read the following account of the lost crew too.**

> Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero who travelled far and wide

Butler, Book 1, opening sentence. **[I]** “Ingenious” selects one aspect of the many-sided Greek epithet *polytropos*. It encourages admiration, but the poem will also show ingenuity becoming deception, suspicion and dangerous self-display. Do not make one English adjective a complete definition of the Greek hero.

**B01-Q2 — Zeus, at the divine council.**

> See now, how men lay blame upon us gods for what is after all nothing but their own folly.

Butler, Book 1, Zeus's first speech. **[I]** This is a character's argument about responsibility, tested by the story. Athena immediately introduces a sufferer whose case complicates it. Treating it as “Homer says every misfortune is deserved” would erase that exchange.

**B01-Q3 — Telemachus receiving the disguised Athena.**

> to our house, and when you have partaken of food you shall tell us what you have come for.

Butler, Book 1, continuation of Telemachus's welcome. **[I]** Assistance precedes interrogation. His first credible sign of fitness to lead is attention to someone waiting at the door, before his speeches about power.

**B01-Q4 — Telemachus describing his inheritance.**

> he is gone without leaving so much as a trace behind him, and I inherit nothing but dismay.

Butler, Book 1, conversation with Mentes. **[I]** An inheritance ought to transmit property and reputation. Here it transmits an unresolved absence. The sentence joins grief to the social problem of succession.

**B01-Q5 — Telemachus speaking to Penelope.**

> for speech is man’s matter,

Butler, Book 1, dismissal of Penelope after her appeal to Phemius. **[I]** This little clause deserves more scrutiny than an inspirational quotation: the young man's emerging public voice comes with an attempt to restrict his mother's voice.

## Important passages and close reading

**Essential: the invocation and the council. [I]** The opening moves from one man to his companions, from survival to failure, from human effort to divine refusal. The narrator initially stresses the crew's culpability; later books supply hunger, bad leadership decisions and conflicting desires. The opening verdict is a framework we must carry into that later evidence, not a reason to stop investigating it. The story also begins after the sack of Troy: the hero's suffering does not erase his position among the conquerors.

**Essential: the Aegisthus–Orestes example. [T/I]** Zeus's example becomes Athena's motivational model for Telemachus. One story teaches another character how to act. The comparison is dangerous as well as useful: Orestes's father is dead, Odysseus is alive; Clytemnestra and Penelope cannot simply be equated. The poem repeatedly introduces an apparent parallel and asks us to notice the difference. Read this scene again beside Books 3, 11 and 24.

**Essential: two forms of eating. [I]** Telemachus feeds a new guest; the suitors feed themselves at a house whose owner cannot defend it. The issue is not pleasure versus abstinence. The ethical distinction lies in consent, reciprocity and limits. A repeated meal can reveal a social order as clearly as a battle. Notice too the workers carrying water, bread and meat: elite generosity depends on labour the feast rarely puts at its centre.

**Essential: the dispute over Phemius. [I/A]** For the suitors, a return-song is entertainment; for Penelope, it reopens grief. Telemachus defends the singer but also silences the person most hurt. One reading sees increasing maturity and protection of the bard; another sees an adolescent performing authority in the one place where he can readily exercise it. Both arise from the same scene. Nor is Phemius a fully free performer: the narrator says the suitors compel him to sing. Book 22 returns to that fact.

**Do not skip: Euryclea's purchase. [T/I]** The narrator describes Laertes buying her for twenty oxen. Her affection for Telemachus is real within the story, and so is her enslavement. Familial language must not erase ownership. The preserved household is both a refuge and a hierarchy; this tension intensifies in Books 17, 19 and 22.

## Myth and cultural context [T/C]

The Muse is addressed as a daughter of Zeus: the poet invokes divine authority for an account of events beyond ordinary experience. Athena's help and Poseidon's hostility are not two parts of an impersonal cosmic justice; they are actions by distinct gods with relationships and grievances. Book 1 explicitly gives Poseidon's connection to Polyphemus. Atlas supports the columns separating heaven and earth in this passage; he is not described as carrying the planet on his shoulders.

*Xenia* names the guest-host relationship illuminated by the welcome scene; *oikos* the household, including people, goods and estate; *nostos* homecoming; *kleos* reputation carried in reports and song. These are working terms, not a philosophical system Homer sets out in definitions. [Gregory Nagy's commentary](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/curated-article/gregory-nagy-a-sampling-of-comments-on-the-iliad-and-odyssey/) is useful for checking particular Greek terms; interpretations here are primarily our own close readings of the cited scenes.

## Philosophical stakes [I]

**Action under uncertainty:** Telemachus need not know the ending to convene an assembly or seek information. Divine support does not make his speaking and travelling unnecessary. **Identity through relationship:** others recognise his resemblance to a father he barely knows; he must decide what to do with an identity transmitted by testimony. **Authority and care:** his welcome earns admiration, while his treatment of Penelope prevents a simple equation of adulthood with moral improvement.

**[M] Modern analogy:** someone coping with an absent family member may alternate between paralysis, gathering information and trying to control the remaining household. That comparison clarifies a pattern; it does not diagnose Telemachus or reduce Athena to an inner voice.

## Questions for our reading

1. Which of Telemachus's actions deserves our confidence before Athena promises him anything?
2. How does Athena's response change the force of Zeus's opening claim about blame?
3. Can the restoration of this household be good for its members in unequal ways?
