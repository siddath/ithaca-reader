# Book XV — Converging Routes: Telemachus, Theoclymenus, and Eumaeus' Past

Primary text: [Samuel Butler, *Odyssey* XV](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.15.xv.html). Local text: `sources/odyssey-15.txt`. Butler's Minerva and Mercury are Athena and Hermes.

## What happens, and who knows what

[T] Athena urges Telemachus to leave Sparta, warns of the suitors' ambush, and sends him first to Eumaeus rather than the palace. Menelaus and Helen give departure gifts. An eagle carrying a domestic goose appears; Helen interprets it as Odysseus returning to take revenge. Telemachus accepts the fugitive seer Theoclymenus, who says he killed a kinsman and is pursued by powerful relatives.

> The chief men among the suitors are lying in wait for you

> The eagle came from the mountain where it was bred and has its nest, and in like manner Ulysses, after having travelled far and suffered much, will return to take his revenge

Helen interpreting the omen; Book XV, searchable phrase “eagle came from the mountain”; local lines 143–149.

[T] At the hut Odysseus continues to test Eumaeus by proposing to beg at the palace. Eumaeus protests, then tells his life: born a prince on Syrie, he was kidnapped by a Phoenician woman enslaved in his father's house; she says Taphian pirates had earlier abducted her from Sidon and sold her. Phoenician traders arrange her flight, during which they also abduct Eumaeus. She dies during the voyage; Laertes buys Eumaeus, and Anticlea raises him with Ctimene before assigning him to the country. Telemachus lands secretly, entrusts Theoclymenus to Piraeus, and goes to the swine farm. A hawk tearing a dove prompts Theoclymenus to predict the endurance of Telemachus' house.

> am an exile, for I have killed a man of my own race.

> he shall find no lack of hospitality.

## The passages to hold onto

[T] Eumaeus' autobiography (local lines 289–422) is the chapter's moral center. It discloses what the loyal “servant” label suppresses: he is a trafficked child whose current household membership began in purchase. It also gives the enslaved Phoenician woman motive, history, sexual vulnerability, and agency while showing how traders exploit all of them.

[T] Theoclymenus' reception (211–241, 429–466) puts Telemachus in the host's role just as he returns to danger. His act protects a homicide fugitive whose account cannot be externally verified. Hospitality does not certify innocence; it creates temporary safety under uncertainty.

[C] Bird omens are narrated as divine signs but still require interpreters. Helen and Theoclymenus speak confidently; their social authority and subsequent plot alignment do not make all omen-reading a mechanical code. Apollo is associated with the hawk in Butler's text.

## Close readings

[I] Book XV braids four returns: Telemachus to Ithaca, Odysseus toward the palace, Eumaeus' remembered removal from home, and the exile Theoclymenus seeking a new refuge. “Home” is distributed unequally. The free elite can recover it; the trafficked child grows into loyal service in the purchaser's estate.

[I] Eumaeus' story disrupts a sentimental household picture. Anticlea's care and Ctimene's companionship were real, and so was purchase. [A] The poem can represent humane treatment relative to other masters without converting enslavement into adoption. Kin-like affection does not erase ownership.

[I] The Phoenician woman's decision to flee is framed by Eumaeus within ethnic suspicion and moral blame, yet her own speech says she was abducted from Sidon and sold by Taphian pirates. [A] She may betray the household that enslaves her, but “faithlessness” is an ethically unstable label for an enslaved person trying to return home. The traders then make her an instrument of another child's abduction.

[I] Telemachus has matured enough to route people and property: he avoids the ambush, sends the crew onward, places Theoclymenus with Piraeus, and heads to Eumaeus. Yet Athena supplies decisive intelligence. Agency and divine aid work together; neither cancels the other.

[I] The omens show interpretation as action. Helen turns a predatory image into a hopeful homecoming forecast; Theoclymenus turns another into dynastic assurance. [A] To the goose and dove, both signs are scenes of death. The same image that promises justice to the household encodes destruction for its target.

## Motifs, links, and modern comparison

[T] Links: Telemachus' hospitality answers earlier lessons from Nestor and Menelaus; the ambush resumes IV and returns in XVI; Eumaeus' origin deepens XIV; bird signs anticipate Theoclymenus' darker vision in XX; converging father and son routes meet in XVI.

[M] Modern analogy: the chapter resembles a secure re-entry plan with a trusted off-site rendezvous. Telemachus does not walk into the compromised headquarters; he separates transport, guest care, valuable goods, and personal movement. Eumaeus' story warns, however, that a well-run household can rest on an old supply chain of trafficking.

## Questions

1. [A] How should we assess the Phoenician woman's flight once her prior abduction and enslavement are centered?
2. [I] What has Telemachus learned about hosting when he accepts Theoclymenus?
3. [I] Why do both favorable omens depict one bird violently taking another?

## Provisional Joyce bridge

[M] Joyce's “Oxen of the Sun” and “Circe” intervene between the Homeric namesakes “Nausicaa” and “Eumaeus,” so a Book XV parallel should remain loose. The useful comparison is narrative convergence: separate urban routes and provisional companions move toward a late-night father-son alignment.
