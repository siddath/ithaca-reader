# Book XVIII — Beggars as Spectacle, Penelope as Strategist

Primary text: [Samuel Butler, *Odyssey* XVIII](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.18.xviii.html). Local text: `sources/odyssey-18.txt`. Butler's Minerva and Jove are Athena and Zeus.

## What happens, and who knows what

[T] Arnaeus, nicknamed Irus because he runs errands, tries to expel the disguised Odysseus from the palace threshold. The suitors convert the conflict between two hungry men into entertainment and threaten the loser with mutilation by Echetus. Odysseus exposes enough strength to frighten Irus and breaks his jaw while deliberately avoiding a killing blow that might reveal him. He drags Irus outside; the suitors laugh.

[T] Odysseus warns Amphinomus to leave before the master's return, but Athena keeps him from doing so. Athena then prompts Penelope to appear. Penelope criticizes Telemachus for permitting abuse of a suppliant and maneuvers the suitors into giving her valuable courtship gifts by stating the proper custom: suitors enrich a prospective bride's household rather than consume it. Odysseus recognizes the strategy. Melantho abuses him; Eurymachus mocks his willingness to work and throws another stool, striking the cupbearer. Telemachus closes the feast.

## The passages to hold onto

[T] The Irus fight (local lines 8–111) is not a neutral athletic contest. Food insecurity is engineered into violence for elite amusement. The suitors supply the prize, threat, and audience; Odysseus participates tactically; Irus bears extreme injury and humiliation.

[T] Penelope's gift speech (222–277) demonstrates agency inside constraint. She invokes custom, loss, and unwanted marriage, then converts the suitors' competition into partial restitution. Her performance is legible to Odysseus but not to the men she flatters.

[C] Courtship gift practices vary across archaic Greek contexts; the text's immediate contrast is clear even if a universal historical rule is not. Penelope frames legitimate wooing as costly to the suitor, unlike the current extraction from her household.

## Close readings

[I] The fight shows horizontal conflict produced by vertical power. Two marginalized beggars are made rivals for access to the same table, while the wealthy household consumers enjoy the spectacle. [A] Irus initiates the threat and has local status as the regular beggar; acknowledging that agency does not absolve the suitors who escalate, reward, and laugh.

[I] Odysseus uses calibrated violence. He chooses not to kill, but a broken jaw and public dragging remain severe. [A] The scene can display self-control relative to lethal capacity; it can also show how easily the hero's tactical needs absorb a poorer man's body.

[I] Penelope's apparent compliance functions as extraction. She neither discloses a settled intention to remarry nor simply refuses; she uses the suitors' own courtship claims to demand material cost. [A] The narrator and Odysseus celebrate her cunning, but her choices remain shaped by pressure from natal family, adult son, household depletion, and hostile guests.

[I] Athena repeatedly intensifies insolence so Odysseus' anger will grow. Divine causation does not make the suitors automatons: they act through existing habits of contempt. Yet it complicates a purely juridical reading in which every provocation independently earns later punishment.

[I] Eurymachus offers labor only to demean the beggar, assuming hunger proves laziness. Odysseus answers with concrete claims about mowing, plowing, and warfare. The exchange contests who may define productive worth, though the hero's answer still culminates in elite martial ability.

## Motifs, links, and modern comparison

[T] Links: bodily appetite echoes IX–XII; the footstool repeats XVII; Penelope's verbal craft parallels Odysseus' Cretan tales; Athena's beautification recalls divine transformations; Telemachus' growing authority advances the plan of XVI.

[M] Modern analogy: the Irus contest resembles a reality program or gig platform that makes precarious workers compete for a meal while spectators call the result merit. Penelope's response resembles charging exploitative bidders a nonrefundable participation cost, recovering value without yet surrendering control.

## Questions

1. [I] Who is most responsible for the violence against Irus?
2. [A] Is Penelope's gift strategy resistance, delay, resource recovery, or all three?
3. [I] What does Odysseus' “lighter blow” reveal about the poem's scale of acceptable violence?

## Provisional Joyce bridge

[M] Joyce's “Circe” makes bodies, humiliation, desire, and spectatorship unstable in hallucinatory performance. Book XVIII offers a precursor in which social power stages identity through public degradation, while strategic performance becomes a means of survival.
