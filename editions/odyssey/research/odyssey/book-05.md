# Book 5 — Choosing a finite home; the hero stripped to survival

Primary: [Butler, Book 5](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.5.v.html); Samuel Butler (1900), [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1727); exact quote source `sources/odyssey-05.txt`.

## What happens [T]

Athena again raises Odysseus's suffering and the danger to Telemachus. Zeus sends Hermes to Calypso, ordering Odysseus's release. Hermes admires the island's beauty; Odysseus is elsewhere, weeping at the shore. Calypso protests the gods' treatment of goddesses who take mortal lovers. She nevertheless obeys and offers materials and provisions. Odysseus first requires an oath that she means him no harm. He tells her he still wants his mortal wife and home despite the superiority of her immortal beauty and her offer of immortality.

He builds a raft and sails. Poseidon sees him and raises a storm. The sea goddess Ino-Leucothea offers a protective veil; Odysseus distrusts the advice to abandon the raft until the craft breaks apart. Athena restrains the winds. He struggles towards a dangerous rocky shore, eventually prays to a river, lands at its mouth, returns the veil and hides under leaves beneath intertwined growth. Athena gives him sleep.

This is our first direct encounter with the hero after four books of other people's expectations. He enters in grief and captivity, then as a craftsman and swimmer, not as a victorious warrior.

## Quotations to mark

**B05-Q1 — Narrator, on the coercive relationship.**

> though he was forced to sleep with her in the cave by night, it was she, not he, that would have it so.

Butler, Book 5, Calypso finding Odysseus after Hermes leaves. **[T]** This passage prevents us from representing the entire stay as an unconstrained romantic holiday. Subsequent intimacy does not cancel the explicitly stated coercion.

**B05-Q2 — Odysseus replying to Calypso.**

> Nevertheless, I want to get home, and can think of nothing else.

Butler, Book 5, discussion of Penelope and immortality. **[I]** He does not win an argument about comparative beauty. He refuses to let comparative beauty decide what he values.

**B05-Q3 — Odysseus anticipating further suffering.**

> If some god wrecks me when I am on the sea, I will bear it and make the best of it.

Butler, Book 5, same speech. **[I]** Commitment does not depend on a promise of comfort. His later terror shows that willingness to bear pain is not the absence of fear.

**B05-Q4 — Odysseus's prayer to the river.**

> Have mercy upon me, O king, for I declare myself your suppliant.

Butler, Book 5, the river-mouth landing. **[I]** Survival finally requires asking for help from a power he cannot even name, not displaying control over everything.

## Important passages and close reading

**Essential: paradise from whose viewpoint? [I]** Hermes is delighted by the cave, vegetation, flowing water and fragrance. The person who cannot leave experiences the same place as imprisonment. The contrast is enacted by his absence from the cave when Hermes enters. Beautiful surroundings do not tell us whether a life is freely chosen. This is a scene about perspective and agency before it is an allegory of temptation.

**Essential: Calypso's protest. [T/I/A]** She cites Dawn with Orion and Demeter with Iasion to accuse the gods of jealous inequality. Her argument exposes a divine sexual double standard. It does not by itself justify keeping an unwilling man. The reader can recognise the injustice she names and the coercion she practises. Making her merely a villain or merely a liberated lover collapses that tension.

**Essential: Penelope, mortality and particular attachment. [I]** Odysseus admits the goddess's advantages and still wants his own home. One philosophical reading finds value in a finite, shared life rather than endlessly continued existence detached from one's human relations. A more narrowly practical reading stresses his specific longing and kingly identity rather than a universal rejection of immortality. The poem gives us his choice; it does not formulate a rule that every person must choose as he does.

**Essential: building the raft. [I]** The extended craft passage restores agency step by step: selecting timber, shaping it, joining it, rigging and launching. Intelligence lives in hands and material judgement as well as speech. Yet the storm destroys the product. Skill remains worthwhile even when it cannot guarantee its result. Calypso's provision of tools and supplies also matters; freedom here is not achieved alone.

**Essential: distrusting rescue. [I/A]** Odysseus requests an oath from Calypso and initially doubts Ino. Suspicion has good reasons in a world of dangerous encounters, but it can delay help. He adopts a conditional plan: keep the raft while it holds, then swim. This is practical judgement under uncertainty, not an illustration of a later formal philosophical school. His repeated internal debates let us watch intelligence work under exhaustion.

**Essential: the body at the shore. [I]** The octopus comparison describes skin torn from his hands by the rocks. His swollen body cannot initially speak or breathe properly. The hero whose name commands armies becomes a vulnerable organism. That physical extremity prepares the encounter with Nausicaa: the question is whether strangers will see only a frightening body or recognise a person entitled to help.

**Do not skip: the final fire-seed simile. [I]** He covers himself in leaves as a solitary person protects an ember in ashes. This is a locally explicit comparison, stronger evidence than a free symbolic association. His surviving life is small, hidden and capable of being rekindled. We can read a return from the edge of death without claiming literal resurrection. The foliage is shelter in the first instance; its possible thematic links to the olive bed in Book 23 remain an interpretation.

## Mythological context [T/C]

Calypso is a nymph and daughter of Atlas in the poem's framing. Ino is identified as a mortal raised to divine status and called Leucothea; the book does not recount her whole later mythic biography. Hermes is divine messenger, while the river is approached as a god in its own right. Nature in this poem is not simply scenery with human metaphors painted on it.

The Styx oath binds Calypso's assurance in the scene. Divine agreement sets the destination without removing every intervening danger. Zeus's authority, Poseidon's anger, Athena's help, Ino's veil and the river's mercy are distinct actions. Avoid turning this plurality into a perfectly consistent modern theory of providence.

## Example, Joyce and discussion [M]

A person might decline an objectively more luxurious life because it excludes relationships that make it their life. That analogy illuminates the choice; it does not make the captive's situation equivalent to choosing between holiday destinations.

Joyce's [“Calypso,” Episode 4](../ulysses/episode-04.md), introduces Bloom in domestic activity. The home that Homer seeks becomes Joyce's starting point, already complicated by desire, habit and marriage. The title is a lens, not a claim that Molly literally imprisons Bloom on an island.

1. What evidence makes the island simultaneously beautiful and intolerable?
2. Does Odysseus choose mortality in general or his particular mortal life?
3. When does his suspicion protect him, and when does it complicate rescue?
