# Book 8 — The hero hears himself become a story

Primary: [Butler, Book 8](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.8.viii.html), Samuel Butler (1900), [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1727); exact quote source `sources/odyssey-08.txt`.

## Narrative [T]

Alcinous arranges the ship and crew for the stranger's return. At a feast the blind singer Demodocus tells of a quarrel between Odysseus and Achilles. Odysseus hides his tears; Alcinous notices and turns the gathering towards athletic contests. Initially unwilling to compete, Odysseus responds to Euryalus's insulting dismissal by throwing a discus far beyond the others and asserting his abilities. Alcinous restores cordiality and turns to the Phaeacians' accomplishments in dance and song.

Demodocus sings how Hephaestus trapped his wife Aphrodite with Ares in a net and exposed them to the gods' laughter. The entertainment continues; Euryalus apologises and gives Odysseus a sword. Gifts are assembled. Nausicaa asks to be remembered and receives a grateful reply. At the next performance Odysseus honours the bard with food and asks for the wooden horse story. Again he weeps. Alcinous stops the performance and finally asks his name, homeland, travels and connection to Troy. Odysseus will answer in Book 9.

## Quotations to mark

**B08-Q1 — Odysseus replying to Euryalus.**

> the gods do not grace all men alike in speech, person, and understanding.

Butler, Book 8, athletic insult scene. **[I]** Bodily beauty, rhetorical skill and judgement do not necessarily coincide. The insult itself provides the immediate example of impressive appearance without tact.

**B08-Q2 — The gods' response to Hephaestus's trap.**

> Ill deeds do not prosper, and the weak confound the strong.

Butler, Book 8, Demodocus's second song. **[I/A]** Craft defeats speed and strength, but the surrounding laughter and Hermes's unabashed desire complicate a straightforward moral lesson.

**B08-Q3 — Nausicaa saying farewell.**

> do not forget me when you are safe at home again,

Butler, Book 8, before the final feast. **[I]** The woman who helped him has a claim on his memory. A return can be completed only through people whom its main story might otherwise leave behind.

**B08-Q4 — Narrator's simile for Odysseus's tears.**

> He wept as a woman weeps when she throws herself on the body of her husband who has fallen before his own city and people,

Butler, Book 8, after the wooden horse song. Read the whole ensuing description of capture and enslavement, not just this beginning. **[I]** The conqueror's distress is made imaginable through the suffering of a conquered woman.

**B08-Q5 — Alcinous explaining heroic suffering.**

> in order that future generations might have something to sing about.

Butler, Book 8, final speech. **[I/A]** A comfortable host offers an explanation that turns suffering into song. The crying survivor sitting beside him makes that explanation troubling rather than self-evidently sufficient.

## Important passages and close reading

**Essential: Demodocus's first song and the concealed tears. [I]** A celebrated event and a remembered experience occupy the same room. The Phaeacians delight in a story whose participant cannot hear it comfortably. The distinction recalls Penelope and Phemius in Book 1. The poem makes the listener's circumstances part of an artwork's ethical meaning. Alcinous's attentiveness matters: he responds to distress before knowing its complete cause.

**Essential: the insult and discus. [I/A]** Odysseus has said that his mind is on returning, not competing. A status insult nevertheless changes his priorities. His superiority becomes visible, and this earns respect, but the vulnerability of heroic pride is visible too. Compare the name shouted to Polyphemus in Book 9. Here the social breach can be repaired through apology, gifts and restraint; elsewhere a triumphant assertion of identity will expose him to a curse.

**Essential: the song of Ares, Aphrodite and Hephaestus. [I]** A wronged husband lays a concealed trap and exposes those who violate his bed. The analogy with the coming Ithacan plot is suggestive, but its differences are as important as its similarities. The gods laugh, negotiate damages and release the lovers; the Ithacan confrontation kills. Hephaestus's craft overcomes a physically formidable rival, echoing the poem's interest in intelligence and technique. The joke does not provide a universal ethical verdict on sexuality, disability or marital betrayal.

**[A] Counterreading:** some listeners may hear a light interlude, others a pointed miniature of household conflict. It need not have one coded correspondence for every character. Poseidon negotiates rather than punishes here, another reminder that a god's role changes with the relation and situation.

**Essential: the wounded victor in the captive-woman simile. [I]** The simile does not explicitly say Odysseus regrets the war or confesses guilt. It does place his body and emotion into the position of people on the other side of conquest. The woman sees her husband die defending home and children; she is then beaten and enslaved. Reading only “a great warrior cries” misses the political and gendered crossing. Reading it as a complete modern anti-war manifesto goes beyond what is stated. Its force comes from letting the victor's pain be represented through an enemy's possible pain without resolving the contradiction.

**Essential: requesting a painful song. [I]** Odysseus actively asks for the horse, rather than merely enduring an accidental reminder. He tests the bard's accuracy and encounters his own public identity. The poem stages a relation between witness and singer: the witness recognises what the singer renders through inspired art. We should not infer that Demodocus proves Homer's personal blindness or eyewitness status.

**Do not skip: the escort's latent danger. [T/I]** Alcinous recalls a prophecy of Poseidon's anger at Phaeacian conveyance of strangers. He still offers help. This prepares Book 13 and complicates the notion that good hospitality is always rewarded. Phaeacian ships are described as knowing their destination; that is a marvel of this story-world, not evidence of an ancient technological device.

## Myth, poetry and philosophy [T/C/I]

Demodocus receives song and blindness together in the narrator's account of the Muse's gifts. Divine favour can be mixed; artistic excellence does not require that we romanticise real disability as a special gift. The episode belongs to a poem shaped by traditions of performance. Gregory Nagy's [discussion of oral traditions and authorship](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/curated-article/gregory-nagy-oral-traditions-written-texts-and-questions-of-authorship/) provides a scholarly account of composition and recomposition; there is debate about the precise path from performance to our written text.

The central philosophical question is what representation does to lived experience. Song preserves fame but abstracts events from the bodies that suffered them. The listener can admire its accuracy and find it unbearable. Alcinous's interruption models a humane limit: the entertainment of the group does not automatically override the pain of one guest.

**[M] Example:** an account of a historic achievement can be celebratory for the audience and painful for a participant who remembers deaths, compromises or losses omitted from the celebration. That is an analogy about perspective, not a retrospective medical diagnosis of Odysseus.

## Joyce and questions [M]

Music, literary self-consciousness and conflicting perspectives recur throughout Joyce, especially [“Sirens,” Episode 11](../ulysses/episode-11.md). But Demodocus is not simply Joyce, and Odysseus's hidden identity is not a template that fixes every modern character's role.

1. Why does the poem place a comic adultery song between two painful heroic songs?
2. What can the captive-woman simile make us recognise that a direct statement could not?
3. Does fame compensate for suffering, preserve it, exploit it, or do several of these at once?
