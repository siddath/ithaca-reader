# Book XVII — Argos at the Threshold: The House Judges Its Master

Primary text: [Samuel Butler, *Odyssey* XVII](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.17.xvii.html). Local text: `sources/odyssey-17.txt`. Butler's Jove is Zeus.

## What happens, and who knows what

[T] Telemachus returns to the palace, reunites with Penelope, and reports the limited news gathered abroad while withholding Odysseus' return. Theoclymenus declares that Odysseus is already on Ithaca. Meanwhile Eumaeus leads the disguised Odysseus toward town. The goatherd Melanthius abuses and kicks him. At the palace gate the neglected dog Argos recognizes his master, wags, and dies; Odysseus hides a tear.

[T] Inside, Telemachus instructs the stranger to beg from each suitor. Most give food. Antinous attacks Eumaeus for bringing a beggar, refuses bread that is not his, and throws a footstool at Odysseus. Odysseus remains still and invokes avenging powers; even the other suitors recognize that attacking a stranger may offend a disguised god. Penelope hears and requests an interview, but Odysseus delays until evening to avoid the suitors.

## The passages to hold onto

[T] Argos' recognition (local lines 254–282) is the smallest and perhaps most complete recognition scene. No proof is demanded and no tactical alliance follows. The dog's body registers continuity that the human household cannot yet acknowledge. Recognition arrives together with death, so the master receives love without the possibility of repair.

[T] The begging circuit and assault (292–425) turn hospitality into a diagnostic. Odysseus gathers evidence one host at a time. Antinous' offense is intensified because the goods he withholds and weaponizes belong to Odysseus.

[C] Beggars and strangers occupy a protected but precarious position under Zeus. The suitors themselves mention gods traveling in disguise, so ignorance of the norm is not their defense. Knowledge without practice becomes part of the indictment.

## Close readings

[I] The house is tested by its least powerful apparent visitor. Rank is hidden, so conduct toward the beggar reveals what status usually conceals. [A] This is not a modern egalitarian test: some suitors give food partly because they fear a god, and Odysseus' true elite identity shapes the dramatic irony. Still, the poem makes treatment of a vulnerable stranger morally probative.

[I] Argos is both animal witness and material evidence of failed stewardship. His body lies on manure, full of fleas, while the suitors consume the estate. Eumaeus blames enslaved workers' neglect, saying Zeus removes half a person's virtue in slavery. [A] That explanation recognizes the degrading force of enslavement but also voices a master's stereotype about coerced labor. The estate's disorder cannot simply be laid on enslaved women.

[I] Odysseus' endurance is strategic, not passive. The blow is stored as evidence and future motive. His rock-like stillness contrasts with internal revenge. [A] Self-command prevents premature failure, but a reader should ask whether every later killing follows from individually tested guilt or whether accumulated rage broadens punishment.

[I] Telemachus performs authority while constrained. He can order that the beggar receive food and rebuke Antinous, but cannot yet stop violence. His irony—Antinous cares for him “as though I were your son”—uses speech to expose predation without revealing the plan.

[I] Penelope knows the beggar may carry news but does not know he is Odysseus. Telemachus knows the father is present but withholds it from her. The household's emotional center is therefore surrounded by men rationing information for a plan she did not design.

## Motifs, links, and modern comparison

[T] Links: disguised divinity recalls Athena's appearances; Argos anticipates scar, bed, and orchard signs; thrown stools recur in XVIII; begging tests *xenia* as in XIV; the threshold marks the passage from reconnaissance to judgment.

[M] Modern analogy: a leader returns incognito and evaluates an organization by how staff treat someone they believe has no influence. The method reveals culture, but it also raises a governance problem: secret “mystery shopping” lets the powerful define the test and the consequences without due process.

## Questions

1. [I] Why can Argos recognize Odysseus without a test when the humans cannot?
2. [A] Does fear that a beggar may be a god ground genuine hospitality, or only prudent self-protection?
3. [I] What evidence is Odysseus collecting, and what would count as enough to change his plan?

## Provisional Joyce bridge

[M] Joyce's “Wandering Rocks” disperses attention across Dublin rather than centering one threshold. Argos nonetheless offers a useful Joycean lens: overlooked bodies and minor observers can register a truth before socially powerful characters do.
