# Book XXIII — The Rooted Bed: Recognition After Bloodshed

Primary text: [Samuel Butler, *Odyssey* XXIII](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.23.xxiii.html). Local text: `sources/odyssey-23.txt`. Butler's Minerva, Jove, and Neptune are Athena, Zeus, and Poseidon.

## What happens, and who knows what

[T] Euryclea wakes Penelope and reports Odysseus' return and the suitors' deaths. Penelope initially rejects the claim, then considers a god more plausible than a returned husband. She knows neither the plan nor the fighting firsthand. The scar convinces Euryclea, but Penelope insists on her own test. She sits apart, studies the stranger, and tells Telemachus that husband and wife share private tokens.

> there are tokens with which we two are alone acquainted, and which are hidden from all others.

Penelope to Telemachus; Book XXIII, searchable phrase “tokens with which”; local lines 101–106.

[T] Odysseus first manages the political aftermath: music and dancing imitate a wedding so neighbors will not spread news before his party can leave town. Athena restores his appearance, yet Penelope still withholds recognition. She orders Euryclea to move Odysseus' bed outside the chamber. He angrily explains that the bed cannot be moved intact because he built the room around a living olive and fashioned its stump into the bedpost. Penelope now embraces him and explains her fear of deception.

> There was a young olive growing within the precincts of the house, in full vigour, and about as thick as a bearing-post.

Odysseus to Penelope; Book XXIII, searchable phrase “young olive”; local lines 166–175.

> We have suffered, both of us. Heaven has denied us the happiness of spending our youth, and of growing old, together

Penelope to Odysseus; Book XXIII, searchable phrase “We have suffered”; local lines 188–200.

> As the sight of land is welcome to men who are swimming towards the shore

Narrator comparing Penelope's welcome of Odysseus; Book XXIII, searchable phrase “sight of land”; local lines 207–213.

[T] Athena extends the night. The couple go to bed, after which Odysseus recounts the wanderings and Penelope recounts her suffering. He also tells her Tiresias' future demand to carry the oar inland. At dawn he leaves armed for Laertes' farm and instructs Penelope to remain upstairs while news of the killings spreads.

## The passages to hold onto

[T] Penelope's silence and token claim (local lines 81–117) restore her as an evaluator rather than a reward. Telemachus calls her hard, but she has lived for years amid false reports and pressure. Her delayed recognition is evidence-sensitive care.

[T] The bed test (159–205) is significant because the token is both secret knowledge and built environment. Marriage is not proved by appearance; it is proved by knowing the shared structure one partner made and both inhabited. Moving the bed would mean severing the olive's roots.

[C] Olive trees carry practical, economic, and sacred associations across Greek culture, but the passage first gives a concrete construction history. Symbolic readings should begin with carpentry: Odysseus builds the room around an in-situ tree, removes its crown and dresses the rooted trunk into a bedpost. The text does not establish whether the worked stump remains biologically alive.

## Close readings

[I] Penelope's test matches Odysseus' own testing habit but reverses the power. He has examined Eumaeus, Philoetius, servants, suitors, and wife while withholding identity; now she withholds acceptance until he exposes knowledge involuntarily through anger. [A] His anger can confirm authenticity because it is hard to stage, yet it also reminds us that reunion does not remove volatility.

[I] The bed unites rootedness and artifice. Its post remains rooted in place and is crafted above by human skill. Reading the rooted stump as “living growth” is a metaphorical extension, not a botanical fact. Their marriage likewise survives through continuity and active construction, not untouched purity.

[I] The landfall simile assigns the shipwrecked role to Penelope's perception: Odysseus is welcome to her as land to exhausted swimmers. She too has undergone a *nostos*-like survival while remaining physically home. Return belongs to the one who waited as well as the traveler.

[I] Their reunion happens after servants and suitors have died downstairs. Music disguises massacre as marriage. [A] The deception is tactically prudent, but the juxtaposition refuses a purely private happy ending; the household's recovered bed rests within unresolved civic violence.

[I] Penelope attributes Helen's act to divine influence while still calling it wrong. Her comparison expresses fear of being deceived and anxiety about female reputation. [A] It should not be treated as the poem's final universal theory of Helen, agency, or sexual fidelity.

[I] Odysseus immediately says their troubles are unfinished. The oar journey means Poseidon's conflict and the ethical reach of seafaring remain beyond the marriage bed. Recognition restores relation, not immunity from consequence.

## Motifs, links, and modern comparison

[T] Links: Ithacan soil recognized in XIII becomes the rooted bed; weaving and carpentry pair Penelope's and Odysseus' crafts; signs progress from name, scar, skill, and private token; the sea simile imports wandering into the bedroom; Laertes requires another proof in XXIV.

[M] Modern analogy: a couple reunites after a long identity-threatening absence. Public credentials and biometric proof are insufficient; the decisive verification is a shared architectural secret embedded in the home. Authentication becomes relationship because the “password” is a history they built together.

## Questions

1. [I] Why does Penelope trust the bed token more than scar, appearance, or testimony?
2. [A] What does Odysseus' anger contribute to recognition, and what risk does it reveal?
3. [I] Can the private reunion be complete while the deaths remain politically unresolved?

## Provisional Joyce bridge

[M] Joyce's “Penelope” grants Molly the final extended interior discourse, shifting authority from a husband's successful return to a wife's associative consciousness. Homer's bed and Joyce's bed both hold memory, sexuality, betrayal, endurance, and assent, but the narrative access and power distribution differ sharply.
