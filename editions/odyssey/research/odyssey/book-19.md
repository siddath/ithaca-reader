# Book XIX — The Scar, the Web, and Penelope's Unsettled Dream

Primary text: [Samuel Butler, *Odyssey* XIX](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.19.xix.html). Local text: `sources/odyssey-19.txt`. Butler's Minerva, Jove, and Mercury are Athena, Zeus, and Hermes.

## What happens, and who knows what

[T] Odysseus and Telemachus remove the palace weapons while Athena supplies supernatural light; Telemachus gives the planned smoke explanation. Penelope then questions the disguised Odysseus. She narrates the shroud ruse: weaving Laertes' funeral cloth by day and undoing it at night until maidservants disclosed it. Odysseus replies as “Aethon” with another Cretan story, embedding accurate details about Odysseus' clothing and companion. Penelope tests the account through those details, weeps, and hears a near return predicted.

> I used to keep working at my great web all day long, but at night I would unpick the stitches again by torch light.

Penelope to the disguised Odysseus; Book XIX, searchable phrase “unpick the stitches”; local lines 125–140.

[T] Penelope orders Euryclea to wash the stranger's feet. Odysseus recognizes the danger: the old nurse may feel his boar scar. A long account recalls his naming by Autolycus and the hunt on Parnassus. Euryclea recognizes the scar by touch; Odysseus grips her by the throat, demands secrecy, and threatens not to spare her when he later kills disloyal women if she reveals him. Athena diverts Penelope's attention. Penelope recounts the dream of twenty geese killed by an eagle that identifies itself as Odysseus. He gives the obvious interpretation. She remains cautious, distinguishing deceptive ivory dreams from true horn dreams, then announces the bow contest.

> I did not know you till I had actually touched and handled you.

Euryclea to Odysseus; Book XIX, searchable phrase “touched and handled”; local lines 396–403.

> The geese are the suitors, and I am no longer an eagle, but your own husband

The eagle within Penelope's dream; Book XIX, searchable phrase “geese are the suitors”; local lines 455–468.

> dreams are very curious and unaccountable things, and they do not by any means invariably come true.

Penelope to the disguised Odysseus; Book XIX, searchable phrase “dreams are very curious”; local lines 477–483.

## The passages to hold onto

[T] The scar recognition (local lines 318–433) joins name, genealogy, wound, touch, and memory. The scar is not a password detached from life; the narrative reconstructs how a named child became the marked adult. Euryclea's embodied knowledge outruns costume.

[T] The conversation with Penelope (53–316, 433–507) is a duel in partial knowledge. Odysseus knows who both speakers are. Penelope knows her own strategies and memories, suspects stories, and controls the interview. Neither the reader nor Odysseus can reduce her final dream-and-bow decision to a single proven intention.

[C] Autolycus is associated in Homer with theft, oaths, and Hermes. Butler's etymological rendering “Ulysses, or the child of anger” reflects the name's connection with hostility/being angered, but exact etymological claims deserve caution across Greek forms. [U] The scene activates meaningful wordplay without giving a modern linguistic proof.

## Close readings

[I] Penelope and Odysseus both preserve identity through controlled fiction. Her web makes visible progress by day and secretly reverses it by night; his Cretan tale combines invention with verifiable detail. [A] Their symmetry is real but unequal: Penelope deceives coercive suitors to preserve choice, while Odysseus deceives her within a revenge plan from which she is excluded.

[I] Recognition belongs to bodies as well as propositions. Euryclea does not infer from persuasive narrative; she touches a durable wound. Odysseus immediately answers her affection with bodily restraint and a death threat. Private truth is subordinated to operational secrecy through the master's power over an enslaved caregiver who has loved him since infancy. [A] Tactical danger explains why disclosure matters; it does not turn coercion into freely offered loyalty.

[I] The dream supplies a clear allegory and then refuses interpretive closure. Penelope herself narrates the eagle's explanation, so she does not need the stranger's ingenuity. [A] She may be testing him, expressing hope while retaining deniability, or sincerely distrusting dreams. The text does not settle whether she already suspects his identity.

[I] The web's betrayal by maidservants should be read within unequal household conditions. Penelope calls them derogatory names, but their motives and degrees of coercion are not narrated. Later collective punishment must not retroactively turn every uncertain act into freely chosen treason.

[I] The scar is a sign of both vulnerability and elite formation. The boar wound proves continuity, but the hunt was a privileged family rite with maternal kin. Identity is personal, bodily, and socially inherited.

## Motifs, links, and modern comparison

[T] Links: weaving recalls Helen and anticipates Amphimedon's retelling in XXIV; Cretan fiction continues XIII–XIV; the scar returns as proof to Laertes; the eagle repeats XV's omens; the bow contest opens XXI; private recognition recruits Euryclea but forbids disclosure.

[M] Modern analogy: the scar works like a biometric identifier with a full audit trail, while the clothing details function like challenge questions. Biometrics can establish continuity but do not establish present intent; Penelope still needs to know what kind of person has returned.

## Questions

1. [U] Does Penelope suspect the stranger's identity before the bed test, and what evidence remains ambiguous?
2. [I] Why does the poem narrate the scar's entire origin instead of using it as a quick token?
3. [A] How do Penelope's protective deception and Odysseus' tactical deception differ ethically?

## Provisional Joyce bridge

[M] Joyce's “Penelope” radically expands a wife's interior voice that Homer mostly mediates through dialogue and narrator. Book XIX is therefore a productive contrast: Penelope's intelligence is unmistakable, but the text preserves uncertainty about thought by making speech, dream, and test strategically indirect.
