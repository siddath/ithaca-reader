# Book XII — Fatal Knowledge: Sirens, Scylla, and the Cattle of the Sun

Primary text: [Samuel Butler, *Odyssey* XII](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.12.xii.html). Local text: `sources/odyssey-12.txt`. Butler's Jove and the Sun/Sun-god correspond to Zeus and Helios.

## What happens, and who knows what

[T] Back at Aeaea, the crew buries Elpenor. Circe then gives Odysseus a route: Sirens, a choice between dangerous passages, and the cattle of Helios. Odysseus tells the crew how to survive the Sirens and asks to be bound to the mast, but withholds Scylla's inevitable taking of six men. He also disregards Circe's warning not to arm against the immortal monster.

[T] The men plug their ears with wax and tighten Odysseus' bonds when he orders release. At the strait, Charybdis terrifies them while Scylla snatches six. Odysseus calls it the most pitiable sight of his voyage. On Thrinacia winds confine the ship. Supplies fail; while Odysseus is away praying and falls asleep, Eurylochus persuades the starving crew to slaughter Helios' cattle, preferring death at sea to starvation. The hides crawl and meat lows. Zeus destroys the ship; Odysseus alone survives, passing Charybdis again and reaching Calypso.

## The passages to hold onto

[T] The Siren instructions and performance (local lines 39–52, 139–175) offer a rare precommitment design: the future desiring self is expected to revoke the present plan, so authority is delegated to companions who cannot hear the temptation. The bonds do not defeat desire; they keep desire from becoming action.

[T] The Scylla passage (194–222) must be read with the withheld briefing. Odysseus alone knows the planned sacrifice. The six call his name while dying. The simile compares them to fish deceived by bait, temporarily placing heroic sailors in the position of vulnerable prey.

[C] Sirens in this poem are lethal singers surrounded by bones; the familiar later image of mermaid bodies is not specified here. Scylla's genealogy and iconography expand in later myth. Circe presents her as an immortal force that cannot be fought; Odysseus' armor enacts an heroic script the situation has already made useless.

## Close readings

[I] Siren song promises comprehensive knowledge of Troy and “all that happens.” Its threat is not falsehood alone; it is knowledge severed from return. Nagy reads the Sirens as a rival, endless song of Troy that could arrest Odysseus' *nostos* and therefore the *Odyssey* itself. [A] Their song may be truthful in content. The corpses show that true information can still be ruinous when its consumption destroys every other commitment.

[I] Odysseus' selective disclosure at Scylla raises a leadership problem. Full knowledge might paralyze the rowers and kill everyone; secrecy keeps the ship moving but denies six men any voice about their sacrifice. [A] A consequential defense says the alternative is Charybdis and total loss. A rights-based counterreading says necessity does not erase the moral injury of choosing for others while protecting oneself with knowledge and armor.

[I] The cattle episode complicates blame. The crew has been warned repeatedly, yet prolonged starvation narrows meaningful choice. Eurylochus proposes sacrifice and a future temple, trying to convert theft into ritual exchange. Helios refuses that reframing. [A] The act remains a transgression within the poem's divine order; it also grows from material desperation, not cartoon appetite.

[I] Odysseus survives partly because he is absent and asleep when the cattle are killed. The poem's opening blames the companions for destroying themselves, but Book XII shows their decision amid confinement, hunger, hierarchy, and the leader's recurring sleep. Responsibility is real and distributed unevenly.

[I] The gods are agents, not psychological projections: Circe knows routes, Helios demands redress, and Zeus enforces it. Readers may explore what divine action means for human responsibility, but reducing every deity to a mental state deletes the narrative's causal world.

## Motifs, links, and modern comparison

[T] Links: binding answers the forced restraint of IX; appetite completes the crew's long collapse; Odysseus' sleep repeats X; the last ship and companions disappear, creating the isolated survivor of V; the Sirens' Troy-song links backward to heroic fame and forward to Odysseus' successful homecoming story.

[M] Modern analogy: the mast plan resembles asking a trusted system to lock an account during a known period of impaired judgment, with an instruction that later requests to disable the lock are evidence the lock is working. The Scylla decision resembles emergency triage, except the people bearing the loss were not told the plan—precisely the ethical remainder that “least harm” language can conceal.

## Questions

1. [I] Why does Odysseus disclose the Sirens but conceal Scylla?
2. [A] Is Eurylochus' cattle speech impious rationalization, a starving person's tragic reasoning, or both?
3. [I] What kind of knowledge supports homecoming, and what kind arrests it?

## Provisional Joyce bridge

[M] Joyce's “Sirens” makes musical form itself the seductive medium, while “Scylla and Charybdis” stages competing literary theories and the difficulty of navigating between totalizing accounts. The structural bridge is constrained passage through voices that can absorb the traveler; it does not supply a fixed character map.
