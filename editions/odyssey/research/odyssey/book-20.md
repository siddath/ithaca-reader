# Book XX — The Last Feast: Endurance, Labor, and Competing Signs

Primary text: [Samuel Butler, *Odyssey* XX](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.20.xx.html). Local text: `sources/odyssey-20.txt`. Butler's Minerva, Jove, and Diana are Athena, Zeus, and Artemis.

## What happens, and who knows what

[T] Odysseus lies awake, enraged as some palace women go to meet the suitors. He restrains himself by recalling the Cyclops cave. Athena promises support and gives sleep. Penelope wakes and prays to Artemis for death rather than forced marriage, then recalls a dreamlike likeness of Odysseus beside her. Husband and wife occupy the same house, each hearing or imagining the other without shared knowledge.

[T] Odysseus asks Zeus for signs. Thunder sounds from a clear sky, and an exhausted enslaved miller prays that this be the suitors' last feast. Eumaeus arrives warmly; Melanthius insults Odysseus; the cattle herder Philoetius honors the stranger and laments the absent master. The suitors abandon a renewed murder plan after an unfavorable bird sign but continue abuse. Ctesippus throws an ox foot at Odysseus; Telemachus rebukes him. Athena sends unnatural laughter and bloodlike portents. Theoclymenus sees darkness, tears, blood, ghosts, and a blotted sun; mocked, he leaves.

## The passages to hold onto

[T] Odysseus' self-address (local lines 10–54) makes endurance an internal command. The animal simile first makes his heart a protective bitch; the cooking simile then makes his turning body resemble food over flame. Predator, guardian, and potential meat coexist.

[T] The miller's prayer (98–117) anchors divine signs in exploited labor. Thunder alone is ambiguous; her words interpret the day through bodily exhaustion caused by the suitors' consumption. The sign's human content comes from below stairs.

[C] Omen scenes combine divine agency, interpreter, and interested audience. The suitors can heed one bird sign enough to suspend an ambush but laugh away Theoclymenus' vision. Selective belief follows desire; divination does not eliminate interpretation.

## Close readings

[I] Endurance here is neither Stoicism nor a later philosophical doctrine. It is Odysseus' situated skill of delaying action until conditions favor it, explicitly learned from an earlier survival scene. [A] Restraint is valuable, but its object is a planned mass killing. Self-mastery does not by itself guarantee a just end.

[I] Penelope's death wish should be heard as evidence of acute coercive pressure, not romantic proof of perfect fidelity. She faces an unwanted marriage, prolonged uncertainty, and household occupation. [A] Her words belong to a prayer in extremity; they should not be converted into a timeless judgment on remarriage or female virtue.

[I] The miller-woman supplies a class analysis of appetite: each banquet means someone else's night labor. Her weakness has kept her grinding after the others sleep. Divine thunder becomes ethically legible when paired with her workload.

[I] The suitors are not devoid of perception. They recognize ominous birds, forced laughter, and Telemachus' new authority, but repeatedly choose the interpretation that preserves the feast. Their failure is motivated misreading as much as ignorance.

[I] Theoclymenus' vision turns the festive room into its near future. [A] Whether read as literal prophetic sight or poetic prolepsis, it does not prove each person's individual guilt. It marks the group scene as death-bound while Book XXII must still be examined case by case.

[I] Women associated with the suitors are described through Odysseus' angry perception before their individual circumstances are known. The text later includes both accusation that women were forced and charges of disloyalty. Readers should resist resolving that tension prematurely.

## Motifs, links, and modern comparison

[T] Links: the Cyclops memory joins wandering endurance to domestic revenge; sleepless Penelope echoes Odysseus; the final feast reverses *xenia*; servant testimony prepares Eumaeus and Philoetius' recognition; blood visions anticipate XXII and the second underworld in XXIV.

[M] Modern analogy: a failing organization may show dashboard alerts, worker complaints, and expert warnings at once. Leaders who accept only the signal that permits business as usual are not lacking data; they are governing interpretation to protect consumption.

## Questions

1. [I] What does the miller-woman add that Zeus' thunder alone cannot say?
2. [A] When does endurance cease to be a virtue and become preparation for disproportionate revenge?
3. [I] Why can the suitors respond to one omen and mock another?

## Provisional Joyce bridge

[M] Joyce's “Circe” saturates ordinary space with visions, guilt, desire, and judgment; Book XX similarly lets the visible feast coexist with an anticipated death-scene. The comparison concerns layered perception, not an assertion that Homeric gods are psychological hallucinations.
