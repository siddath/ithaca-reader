# Book 4 — Helen's stories, Proteus's transformations, and news that travels slowly

Primary: [Butler, Book 4](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.4.iv.html); Samuel Butler (1900), [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1727), local `sources/odyssey-04.txt`. Quotation locators are the searchable words and named scene.

## Narrative and knowledge [T]

Telemachus and Peisistratus arrive at Menelaus's splendid home during marriage celebrations. Menelaus insists on welcoming them, remembering the help he received on his own travels. Telemachus admires the wealth; Menelaus answers with the losses it cannot repair. Helen recognises Telemachus's resemblance to Odysseus. All four mourn people lost or absent after the war.

Helen puts a grief-banishing drug into their wine and recounts how she recognised Odysseus disguised in Troy and protected his secret. Menelaus follows with the story of Helen circling the wooden horse and imitating the Greek warriors' wives: Odysseus prevented the hidden men from responding. The next morning Menelaus explains how, stranded near Egypt, he captured the shape-changing sea god Proteus with help from Proteus's daughter Eidothea. Proteus told him to perform sacrifices and supplied news of the returning Greeks, including Odysseus's captivity with Calypso.

Telemachus tactfully refuses the gift of horses unsuitable for Ithaca. The narrative then returns to Ithaca, where the suitors discover his departure and plan an ambush. Medon informs Penelope. Euryclea admits helping Telemachus, and Penelope prays. Athena sends a dream in her sister's likeness, reassuring her about the son but declining to answer about the husband. The suitors sail to their ambush.

## Quotations to mark

**B04-Q1 — Menelaus on wealth and loss.**

> so that I have no pleasure in being lord of all this wealth.

Butler, Book 4, Menelaus's first account of his travels. **[I]** The palace's splendour and its owner's experience are not equivalent. Telemachus sees a successful outcome; Menelaus remembers its cost.

**B04-Q2 — Narrator on Helen's drug.**

> She drugged the wine with an herb that banishes all care, sorrow, and ill humour.

Butler, Book 4, before Helen's war story. **[I/A]** Is this compassionate relief, management of an uncomfortable conversation, or both? The action permits storytelling without tears but does not establish that the remembered events have been reconciled.

**B04-Q3 — Menelaus on holding Proteus.**

> but we stuck to him and never lost hold,

Butler, Book 4, Proteus changing shapes. **[I]** Persistence produces knowledge here, but only after a goddess supplies the strategy. Endurance is organised and assisted, not mere stubbornness.

**B04-Q4 — Telemachus on Ithaca.**

> the country is more fit for goats than horses, and I like it the better for that.

Butler, Book 4, declining the horse gift. **[I]** He can admire another place without making it the measure of his own home's worth. This anticipates Odysseus's choice of mortal home over Calypso's splendour.

**B04-Q5 — The dream refusing Penelope further certainty.**

> I shall not tell you for certain whether he is alive or dead,

Butler, Book 4, dream of Iphthime. **[I]** Divine communication can be selective. Reassurance is not omniscience transmitted to every deserving person.

## Important passages and close reading

**Essential: Helen and Menelaus tell stories about the same absent man. [I]** Helen's story places her on the Greek side, recognising and assisting Odysseus. Menelaus's places her dangerously near exposing the Greeks. He acknowledges her preceding story rather than plainly calling it false. The two accounts create unease through juxtaposition: what does it mean for this couple to share a home after their history? Odysseus's restraint is praised in both, but Helen's position shifts.

**[A] Limit:** this is not sufficient evidence to declare that Helen is lying or that Menelaus intends a deliberate verbal attack. She attributes her earlier departure to Aphrodite; he attributes her action near the horse to divine influence. Those explanations may ease blame, reveal their understanding of events, or invite scepticism. Their motives remain interpretive questions.

**Essential: the drug and the cost of memory. [I]** Helen's potion controls the affect of recollection. The poem does not say it erases the facts. Compare the Lotus-eaters in Book 9, whose food destroys the desire to return, and the Sirens in Book 12, who offer knowledge that halts the journey. Memory is not automatically good, nor forgetting automatically evil; its relation to action and relationship matters.

**Essential: Proteus. [I]** A being with fixed knowledge takes unstable visible forms. Menelaus must persist through appearances to obtain an answer. This supports a metaphor of inquiry surviving distraction, but the literal story is a capture involving seal skins, ambrosia, animal smell, coordinated companions and a divine helper. Keep its physical comedy and power struggle alongside the abstraction. Joyce's “Proteus” will turn changing appearance toward perception and language rather than retell the capture.

**Essential: different fates of returning men. [T/I]** The lesser Ajax survives shipwreck and then perishes after boasting against the gods; Agamemnon reaches home and is murdered; Odysseus remains alive but cannot return. These are not interchangeable failures. Arrival, survival, hospitality and knowledge must align for a successful return. The Ajax here is not the great Ajax whose silent shade meets Odysseus in Book 11.

**Essential: Penelope receives danger while Telemachus receives knowledge. [I]** The switch of location makes the successful educational journey costly to someone left behind. Euryclea's dilemma is especially sharp: she has sworn secrecy to the son and now must answer the mother. Penelope's language of possible punishment reveals the hierarchy inside affectionate service. The reader can understand competing loyalties without pretending all participants possess equal freedom.

## Mythological context [T/C]

Proteus is the old man of the sea, associated here with Egypt and Poseidon; Eidothea is his daughter. The god's transformations are literal within the narrative. Helen's divine parentage explains Menelaus's promised Elysian destination: Proteus explicitly links it to his marriage and status as Zeus's son-in-law. This is not a universal doctrine that all virtuous people receive a pleasant afterlife. Compare the much bleaker shades in Book 11.

The wooden horse is recounted here; it is not an episode narrated in the surviving *Iliad*, which ends before Troy falls. The poem assumes a wider Trojan story-world and selects fragments needed for the present scene. The names of Egypt and other distant lands also indicate a world of exchange, travel and marvel. Treat the geography as poetic representation rather than a verified expedition log.

## Philosophical stakes and example [I/M]

The book asks how knowledge arrives through people with mixed motives, partial access and painful memories. Menelaus reports what a god told him; Telemachus receives this as news; Penelope does not receive it at the same moment. Truth existing somewhere is different from truth being available to the person who needs it.

**[M] Example:** two family members can tell accurate stories about the same crisis that imply very different assessments of responsibility. Listening requires attention to selection, sequence and silence, not merely hunting for a factual contradiction.
