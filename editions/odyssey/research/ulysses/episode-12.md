# Episode 12 — “Cyclops”

**Scope.** “Cyclops” is a title from Joyce’s private schemas, retained here as a research label; interpretive comparisons remain provisional. Locators refer to `sources/ulysses-joyce.txt` by searchable phrase and full-text source line number.

## Story and technique

[T] An unnamed narrator meets Joe Hynes and enters Barney Kiernan’s pub, where the Citizen, Alf Bergan, and others debate nationalism, history, sport, race, religion, and Ireland. Bloom tries to argue for a civic definition of nation and for love rather than hatred. The Citizen responds with increasingly violent chauvinism; Bloom leaves, and the Citizen hurls a biscuitbox after the departing car.

[T] The episode alternates colloquial narration with inflated parodies of legal, biblical, heroic, and journalistic prose. [I] The shifting styles expose how political speech can make itself enormous. The pub’s social warmth and its exclusionary violence occupy the same room.

## Important passages and close reading

[T] Bloom’s definition of nation is immediately mocked, then revised by him to include people living in different places. [I] The revision matters: nation is presented as a relation among people rather than a single blood, faith, or territory. It is imperfect and vulnerable, but it gives the episode a different political vocabulary.

[T] The Citizen’s rhetoric repeatedly enlarges bodies, heroes, and enemies until the style itself becomes monstrous. [I] The “one eye” implied by the Homeric tag is a useful description of this narrowing vision, but it should remain an interpretation rather than a claim that the Citizen has only one meaning.

## Homeric echo and crucial difference

[C] The pub’s Citizen echoes Polyphemus in *Odyssey* Book 9: an aggressive host, a cave-like social enclosure, and a conflict with a mobile outsider. The nationalist set pieces also parody heroic boasting.

[I] Bloom is not a Greek raider and does not defeat the Citizen through a planned trick. He leaves because civic argument cannot overcome immediate violence. The episode’s difference is political: the monster is a social voice that ordinary people can repeat, not an isolated supernatural creature.

## Reading question

Can Bloom’s language of love and shared place survive in a room where belonging is defined by exclusion?
