# Episode 17 — “Ithaca”

**Scope.** “Ithaca” is a title from Joyce’s private schemas, retained here as a research label; interpretive comparisons remain provisional. Locators use `sources/ulysses-17.txt`, with global line numbers cross-referenced by `sources/ulysses-boundaries.json`.

## Story and technique

[T] Bloom and Stephen walk to 7 Eccles Street, discussing music, literature, religion, Ireland, sexuality, and the day’s events. Bloom makes cocoa and brings Stephen into the garden. They urinate together under the stars. Stephen refuses Bloom’s invitation to stay and departs; Bloom enters the house, thinks about Molly and the day, and goes to bed.

[T] The episode is organized as catechism: questions and answers reconstruct movement, objects, bodies, and thoughts with scientific precision and comic disproportion. [I] The form promises total knowledge while repeatedly revealing that intimacy exceeds measurement. Its most important event—the possibility of companionship—cannot be reduced to a final answer.

## Important passages and close reading

[T] The questions catalogue the pair’s similarities and divergences, including their different accounts of religion, work, education, and the day’s collapse. [I] The form turns relation into a problem of evidence: Bloom wants to understand Stephen, but the answers often expose the limits of his knowledge.

[T] The stars and scientific measurements widen the scene from one Dublin house to the universe, while the cocoa, garden, and bodily functions keep it local. [I] Joyce places cosmic scale beside domestic vulnerability without declaring either superior. The two men can share a sky without becoming a permanent household.

## Homeric echo and crucial difference

[C] “Ithaca” recalls Odysseus’ return to his island and the recognition scenes that restore a household. Bloom reaches home after a day’s wandering, and Stephen occupies the narrative position of a possible son.

[I] No secret identity is revealed, no bow is strung, and no suitors are defeated. Stephen leaves before a durable adoption can occur. The Homeric echo marks a desire for home and recognition; the modern difference is that companionship remains temporary and knowledge incomplete.

## Reading question

Does the catechism produce intimacy by naming everything, or show why a person can never be fully answered by facts?
