# Episode 3 — “Proteus”

**Scope.** “Proteus” is a title from Joyce’s private schemas, retained here as a research label; interpretive comparisons remain provisional. Locators refer to `sources/ulysses-joyce.txt` by searchable phrase and full-text source line number.

## Story and technique

[T] Stephen walks alone along Sandymount Strand after leaving the school. He tests what can be known through sight, sound, touch, memory, and language. Midwives, birth, his dead mother, Aristotle, Arius, the sea, a dog, and a letter to the press pass through his thoughts. He lies down, rises, and moves toward Dublin as the tide and the day change around him.

[T] The episode is almost entirely interior monologue. Syntax shifts when attention shifts; foreign terms and half-remembered quotations are not decorative additions but records of thinking in motion. [I] The prose makes perception itself the “shape-changing” event: Stephen does not meet a prophetic Proteus, but his objects become unstable as the mind approaches them.

## Short exact passages

> Ineluctable modality of the visible: at least that if no more, thought through my eyes.

Stephen’s opening meditation; full-text source line 1874, searchable by “ineluctable modality.”

> Shut your eyes and see.

Stephen’s compressed instruction to himself; full-text source line 1882.

> Am I walking into eternity along Sandymount strand?

Stephen’s question while walking the shore; full-text source line 1893.

## Important passages and close reading

[T] Stephen distinguishes the visible from the audible and tests the difference by closing and opening his eyes. [I] The line “Shut your eyes and see” is not a mystical solution; it exposes how sensation is already mixed with memory, language, and expectation. The beach becomes a laboratory where the mind discovers that objects are never simply given.

[T] The midwives and umbilical imagery connect individual thought to bodily generation. Stephen’s theological digressions about substance, fatherhood, and heresy turn the body into a disputed inheritance. [I] His learned vocabulary does not master grief: the dead mother remains an emotional presence that the philosophical language circles without resolving.

[C] In the *Odyssey*, Proteus is a shape-shifting sea divinity who must be held until he returns to a truthful form. [I] Joyce relocates that difficulty inside consciousness. Stephen’s difficulty is not extracting a prophecy from a god but finding whether any stable form can survive the mind’s transformations.

## Homeric echo and crucial difference

[C] The sea, changing forms, and pursuit of knowledge invite the “Proteus” comparison. [I] There is no literal god, capture, or reliable prophecy. Stephen’s stream of thought produces many forms but no final revelation; the episode turns the Homeric encounter into an inquiry into language and perception.

## Reading question

Does Stephen’s shifting language bring him closer to the world, or protect him from having to meet grief and responsibility directly?
