# Episode 11 — “Sirens”

**Scope.** “Sirens” is a title from Joyce’s private schemas, retained here as a research label; interpretive comparisons remain provisional. Locators refer to `sources/ulysses-joyce.txt` by searchable phrase and full-text source line number.

## Story and technique

[T] In the Ormond Hotel bar, Miss Douce, Miss Kennedy, Simon Dedalus, Ben Dollard, Richie Goulding, and Bloom encounter one another around music, drink, flirtation, and memory. Bloom hears songs while thinking about Molly, Blazes Boylan, Rudy, and loss. The episode ends with Bloom leaving amid recurring taps, voices, and bodily sounds.

[T] The technique is musical: fragments recur, words imitate instruments, and motifs return with altered emphasis. [I] The prose does not merely describe music; it makes reading depend on anticipation, echo, interruption, and resonance. Sexual attraction is present, but the episode’s seduction also concerns sound, memory, and the promise of knowledge carried through song.

## Important passages and close reading

[T] The opening reduces narrative orientation to sound before the bar’s social scene becomes clear. [I] This is not a decorative prelude: it asks whether a reader can hear relations before understanding them. Names, objects, and memories enter as musical reprises.

[T] Bloom’s thoughts about “All is lost now,” Molly, and Rudy move beneath the public performance. [I] Music permits grief to appear without becoming a formal confession. The barmaids’ voices are part of the scene’s erotic economy, but Bloom’s listening also reveals a man trying to keep private loss from hardening into hatred.

[C] Joyce’s schemata associate the episode with the Sirens. In Homer, the Sirens promise dangerous knowledge of Troy and the wider world. [I] Reading the episode as only sexual seduction misses its epistemic lure: music claims it can tell the listener what the world has been and what he has lost. [A] Later sexualized readings belong in a separate interpretive layer rather than being treated as the Homeric promise itself.

## Homeric echo and crucial difference

[C] The bar’s voices, songs, and inability to leave recall Odysseus bound before the Sirens in *Odyssey* Book 12. [I] Bloom has no mast, wax, or heroic command. His resistance is ordinary and incomplete: he listens, thinks, and leaves while carrying the song with him. The episode’s musical form is itself the hazard.

## Reading question

What kind of knowledge does the music promise, and why does Bloom’s private grief become clearer through sound than through direct explanation?
