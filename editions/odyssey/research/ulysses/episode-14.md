# Episode 14 — “Oxen of the Sun”

**Scope.** “Oxen of the Sun” is a title from Joyce’s private schemas, retained here as a research label; interpretive comparisons remain provisional. Locators use `sources/ulysses-14.txt`, with global line numbers cross-referenced by `sources/ulysses-boundaries.json`.

## Story and technique

[T] Bloom visits the National Maternity Hospital, where Mina Purefoy is in labor. The doctors bring about a successful delivery, and the news is framed as the birth of a boy. After the medical and student commotion, Bloom leaves the hospital with the others heading toward Burke’s. The narrative moves through successive styles of English prose, from archaic and ecclesiastical diction through nineteenth-century literary forms to modern slang and scientific parody.

[T] The technique makes prose history feel like gestation: sentences lengthen, imitate inherited forms, and give birth to new verbal bodies. [I] The analogy is productive but limited. The episode also exposes how a celebrated experiment in literary reproduction can marginalize the woman whose labor supplies its material scene.

## Important passages and close reading

[T] The opening Latinized prose treats national prosperity as a matter of reproduction, while later passages move through medical history, fetal development, and arguments about infant mortality. [I] The episode’s apparent celebration of birth is shadowed by attempts to measure, regulate, and explain women’s bodies. “Nature” becomes both wonder and administrative problem.

[T] The changes in style are not a neutral history of English. They parody the authority of inherited forms while making the reader feel how language accumulates. [I] Reproduction here includes literary inheritance: each style carries an earlier voice into a new context, where it can be honored, distorted, or made ridiculous.

## Homeric echo and crucial difference

[C] Joyce’s “Oxen of the Sun” echoes the cattle of Helios in *Odyssey* Book 12, whose forbidden consumption brings catastrophe. The maternity hospital supplies a setting of bodily appetite, fertility, and institutional care.

[I] No sacred herd is killed and no divine punishment organizes the episode. The crucial difference is formal: the danger lies in a literary culture consuming inherited styles and speaking over embodied labor. The Homeric tag points toward appetite and consequence without dictating one universal symbol.

## Reading question

How does the episode’s celebration of linguistic birth expose the costs of turning another person’s labor into an artistic experiment?
