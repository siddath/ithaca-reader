# Episode 15 — “Circe”

**Scope.** “Circe” is a title from Joyce’s private schemas, retained here as a research label; interpretive comparisons remain provisional. Locators use `sources/ulysses-15.txt`, with global line numbers cross-referenced by `sources/ulysses-boundaries.json`.

## Story and technique

[T] Stephen and Bloom enter Nighttown after the day’s drinking and wandering. The episode is written as a stage script of hallucinations, transformations, accusations, sexual fantasies, historical tableaux, and reversals of authority. Bloom becomes defendant, mayor, king, mother, and supplicant. Bella Cohen’s scene turns her into “Bello” and Bloom into a feminized, subordinated figure before the authority reverses again. Stephen is confronted by the apparition of his dead mother and smashes a chandelier. Near the end Private Carr strikes Stephen; Bloom picks him up and argues with the police while Corny Kelleher helps disperse the crowd. Bloom then tends the unconscious Stephen and sees a vision of Rudy, Bloom’s dead son. The action passes toward Episode 16.

[T] Stage directions make the unconscious public. [I] The episode does not ask the reader to decide which hallucination is “real.” It shows how social power, guilt, desire, memory, and language can become theatrical bodies that act upon the characters.

## Important passages and close reading

[T] Bloom’s transformations repeatedly expose his care for Molly, Rudy, Stephen, and strangers alongside his vulnerability to public judgment. Bella/Bello is the episode’s most concentrated transformation sequence: gender, class, desire, and punishment become roles that can be imposed and exchanged. [I] The dream does not simply liberate Bloom. It makes every role unstable, including roles that seem protective or benevolent.

[T] The mother apparition joins Catholic prayer, bodily decay, and Stephen’s guilt. When Stephen rejects her command to repent and breaks the chandelier, the act is both rebellion and damage. [I] “Non serviam” is not a clean emancipation: the scene shows that refusing inherited authority can still be driven by the authority’s power over memory.

## Homeric echo and crucial difference

[C] “Circe” recalls the enchantress of *Odyssey* Book 10, who transforms Odysseus’ companions and then becomes a host and guide. Bella/Bello is the clearest local analogue, but there is no single stable sorceress: enchantment is distributed through stage-like hallucinations around the characters’ separate and entangled consciousnesses in a colonial, sexualized city.

[I] There is no single sorceress and no stable heroic return to ordinary identity. Bloom and Stephen are changed by language, police power, memory, and one another; the episode’s difference is that the “magic” is distributed through social life and inner conflict.

## Reading question

What kinds of agency remain possible when the scene turns guilt, desire, and social judgment into characters who can speak back?
