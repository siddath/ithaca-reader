# Episode 9 — “Scylla and Charybdis”

**Scope.** “Scylla and Charybdis” is a title from Joyce’s private schemas, retained here as a research label; interpretive comparisons remain provisional. Locators refer to `sources/ulysses-joyce.txt` by searchable phrase and full-text source line number.

## Story and technique

[T] In the National Library, Stephen debates Shakespeare, Hamlet, paternity, biography, and artistic authority with John Eglinton, A. E., and others. Buck Mulligan arrives and turns the discussion toward mockery. Stephen develops a theory in which Shakespeare’s life, the ghost, and the play’s father-son relations intersect; the group never reaches consensus. Bloom is nearby in the library but does not enter the debate.

[T] The episode’s technique is argumentative rather than scenic. Voices collide, quotations are recontextualized, and Stephen’s theory is both brilliant and unstable. [I] The library becomes a strait between competing interpretive hazards: material biography on one side, metaphysical speculation on the other.

## Important passages and close reading

[T] Stephen’s “theory” links Shakespeare, Hamlet, the ghost, and the father’s uncertain relation to the son. [I] The argument is not a transparent statement of Joyce’s own view. Its force comes from performance: Stephen tries to turn personal uncertainty into a public form that others must hear.

[T] “Paternity may be a legal fiction” is surrounded by language of apostolic succession, biological uncertainty, and theological void. [I] The episode does not solve fatherhood; it shows how intellectual systems can make emotional need brilliant, portable, and evasive at once.

## Homeric echo and crucial difference

[C] The schema’s “Scylla and Charybdis” evokes the passage between two dangers in *Odyssey* Book 12. The library debate stages a comparable choice between fixed material explanation and engulfing idealism.

[I] There are no literal monsters and no navigational decision with a measurable casualty count. Bloom’s absence from the conversation also prevents a simple Stephen–Bloom intellectual pairing. The Homeric label is a productive pressure on the debate, not its complete key.

## Reading question

Does Stephen’s theory create a shared language for fatherhood, or turn the need for relation into another performance of isolation?
