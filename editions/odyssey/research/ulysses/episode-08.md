# Episode 8 — “Lestrygonians”

**Scope.** “Lestrygonians” is a title from Joyce’s private schemas, retained here as a research label; interpretive comparisons remain provisional. Locators refer to `sources/ulysses-joyce.txt` by searchable phrase and full-text source line number.

## Story and technique

[T] Bloom walks across Dublin looking for lunch. He reads religious and commercial notices, feeds gulls from the bridge, notices bodies and poverty, remembers Molly, and finally eats at Davy Byrne’s pub. He avoids a meal at Burton’s because the diners’ animal appetite repels him, then chooses a safer, lighter lunch and continues his errands.

[T] The prose is organized by appetite: food, blood, sacrifice, advertising, smell, and memory interrupt one another. [I] “Lestrygonians” does not turn Dubliners into literal man-eating giants. It makes ordinary consumption visible as a social arrangement in which some bodies are fed, some are displayed, and some are excluded.

## Important passages and close reading

[T] Early images of “the blood of the lamb,” birth, hymen, martyrdom, war, and sacrifice place religious language beside eating. [I] The episode asks what kinds of violence become acceptable once they are hidden inside ritual, commerce, or appetite. Bloom’s Jewish background and his particular appetite decisions make the question personal without making him morally pure; Episode 4 has already shown him eating a pork kidney.

[T] The gull poem is deliberately modest and technically self-conscious; Bloom thinks about rhyme, blank verse, and “the flow of the language.” [I] His small act of feeding is also an act of composition. The poem does not conquer the gulls or the city; it makes a temporary relation in which attention and food circulate.

## Homeric echo and crucial difference

[C] The *Odyssey*’s Laestrygonians are cannibal giants who destroy most of Odysseus’ fleet in Book 10. Joyce’s echo is carried by the vocabulary of eating, crowding, and bodily threat.

[I] The crucial difference is scale and agency. Bloom’s danger is social hostility and disgust, not a supernatural attack. His survival depends on choosing where and how to eat, while the city’s ordinary institutions continue around him.

## Reading question

How does Bloom’s way of eating change the episode’s picture of hunger, hospitality, and social belonging?
