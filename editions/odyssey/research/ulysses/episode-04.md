# Episode 4 — “Calypso”

**Scope.** “Calypso” is a title from Joyce’s private schemas, retained here as a research label; interpretive comparisons remain provisional. Locators refer to `sources/ulysses-joyce.txt` by searchable phrase and full-text source line number.

## Story and technique

[T] Leopold Bloom feeds the cat and goes out to buy a kidney, imagining an eastern journey along the way. Returning, he finds letters, cooks, and brings Molly breakfast. She identifies her correspondent as Boylan and asks about metempsychosis; their conversation is interrupted by the smell of the kidney burning. Bloom eats it after cutting away the burnt portion, reads his daughter Milly’s letter, remembers his dead son Rudy, and later uses the privy while reading a prize story. Practical movements continually open into sexual, religious, commercial, and metaphysical associations.

[T] Free indirect discourse lets objects trigger thought without a formal transition: kidney, cat, letter, and household objects become portals into Bloom’s mental life. [I] Joyce introduces Bloom through care and appetite rather than heroic action. The result is a heroism of attention whose scale is domestic and bodily.

## Important passages and close reading

[T] Two letters establish distinct relationships: Molly receives Boylan’s letter, while Bloom reads Milly’s letter to himself. The breakfast conversation includes Molly’s report about Boylan and Bloom’s attempt to explain metempsychosis. He later eats the kidney and uses the privy. [I] Bodily maintenance, grief, sexual anxiety, and domestic routine occupy the same morning; philosophical speculation grows out of that material setting.

[T] Bloom explains “metempsychosis” as transmigration of souls while Molly is more interested in the word’s practical meaning and the book’s sexual content. [I] The exchange makes philosophy a domestic conversation, imperfectly shared but not thereby trivial. The abstract doctrine matters because Bloom uses it to imagine continuity after death, including Rudy’s lost life.

[T] Bloom’s imagined eastern journey moves through “turbaned faces,” mosques, and a mother calling children home. [I] The fantasy is knowingly provisional—he immediately admits it is probably unlike the reality—and exposes how travel writing, empire, and domestic longing shape one another. The pub puzzle turns the city into a map of temptation and routine rather than a heroic route.

## Homeric echo and crucial difference

[C] “Calypso” recalls the island where Odysseus is held by a goddess before returning home. Bloom’s morning reverses the arrangement: he is already in his house, while Molly’s attention is elsewhere and another man will visit later. [I] The episode uses the echo to question captivity and home rather than simply assigning Molly the role of Calypso.

[I] Bloom’s domestic care also complicates the epic model. His food preparation, empathy toward the cat, and curiosity about other lives make ordinary maintenance a mode of intelligence. He is not a warrior delayed from home; he is a husband whose home is intimate, unstable, and still worth returning to.

## Reading question

What does Bloom’s attention to food, animals, and the body allow the novel to know that an elevated heroic style might miss?
