# Episode 7 — “Aeolus”

**Scope.** “Aeolus” is a title from Joyce’s private schemas, retained here as a research label; interpretive comparisons remain provisional. Locators refer to `sources/ulysses-joyce.txt` by searchable phrase and full-text source line number.

## Story and technique

[T] Bloom enters the newspaper offices to place an advertisement for Alexander Keyes. Editors, reporters, printers, and political talkers circulate around him. Stephen arrives carrying Deasy’s letter; later, amid discussion of rhetoric, he offers a story about two old women on Nelson’s Pillar. Bloom leaves to pursue the advertisement while the office continues producing headlines, anecdotes, and nationalist speech.

[T] The episode interrupts its prose with newspaper-style headings: “GENTLEMEN OF THE PRESS,” “A STREET CORTÈGE,” and many others. [I] These headings behave like gusts of wind: they redirect attention, inflate small events, and make language visibly public. The press can preserve a fact, but it can also turn the fact into a slogan before anyone has considered it.

## Important passages and close reading

[T] The office repeatedly turns speech into layout: headlines, paragraphs, advertisements, and clipped phrases. [I] Joyce exposes the material production of “public opinion.” A sentence is not only what it means; it is also where it is printed, who pays for it, and how quickly it can be repeated.

[T] Bloom’s practical concern with an advertisement is surrounded by grand talk of revolution, empire, and historical memory. [I] His modest commercial errand is not beneath the episode’s politics. It shows how civic life is built from small transactions that rhetoric often overlooks.

## Homeric echo and crucial difference

[C] “Aeolus” recalls the keeper of the winds in *Odyssey* Book 10. The newspaper office becomes a wind machine of competing voices, editorial gusts, and phrases that blow through the city.

[I] Unlike Odysseus, Bloom is not given a bag that contains literal winds, and there is no single storm caused by releasing it. The danger is rhetorical dispersal: public language can carry a person forward, deflect him, or leave him with nothing but noise.

## Reading question

What does the episode suggest is lost when a lived event is turned into a headline before it can become a considered memory?
