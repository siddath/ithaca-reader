# Episode 13 — “Nausicaa”

**Scope.** “Nausicaa” is a title from Joyce’s private schemas, retained here as a research label; interpretive comparisons remain provisional. Locators use `sources/ulysses-13.txt`, with global line numbers cross-referenced by `sources/ulysses-boundaries.json`.

## Story and technique

[T] On Sandymount Strand, Cissy Caffrey and Edy Boardman mind the children while Gerty MacDowell imagines romance, beauty, marriage, and social refinement. Gerty notices Bloom watching from the rocks. During fireworks she leans back, displays her stockings, and understands the encounter through the conventions of sentimental magazines. Bloom masturbates behind the rock. Gerty then rises and walks away, exposing her limp; Bloom observes her, and a separate interior monologue unfolds before he later leaves.

[T] The prose first imitates the lush, moralizing language of popular fiction and then shifts toward Bloom’s more fragmented interior style. [I] The change makes style an ethical instrument: the same event looks like a romance when filtered through Gerty’s fantasy and like a compromised, mixed response when filtered through Bloom’s desire, pity, prejudice, and self-justification. Neither perspective is fully sovereign.

## Important passages and close reading

[T] Gerty’s self-presentation draws on beauty advice, magazine romance, Catholic piety, and the promise of social ascent. [I] Joyce does not make her merely foolish: the prose shows a young woman actively composing a possible self from the cultural materials available to her. Her fantasy has agency, but it is also constrained by class, gender, and the gaze of a stranger.

[T] Bloom’s response moves through arousal, pity, prejudice, and self-justification after the fireworks. The romance narrator’s moralizing judgment supplies a layer of moral rebuke, while Bloom’s later thoughts remain mixed rather than offering a straightforward recognition of harm. [I] The episode holds voyeurism, mutual fantasy, disability, and private sexual release together; a single moral label would flatten the scene’s deliberately unstable knowledge.

## Homeric echo and crucial difference

[C] “Nausicaa” recalls the princess who encounters the shipwrecked Odysseus in *Odyssey* Book 6. Nausicaa gives directions and aid while preserving propriety; she does not escort Odysseus into court. The beach, the young woman, the stranger, and the imagined possibility of renewed social life invite the comparison.

[I] Joyce reverses the rescue plot. Bloom is not a naked hero seeking a court, and Gerty does not direct him toward a household or civic welcome. The meeting is mediated by distance, print culture, and erotic spectacle; it ends in separation rather than hospitality.

## Reading question

Who controls the scene’s imagined heroism—the girl’s fantasy, Bloom’s gaze, or the prose that keeps changing between them?
