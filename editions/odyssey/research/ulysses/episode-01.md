# Episode 1 — “Telemachus”

**Scope.** This is a provisional interpretive map using Homeric names from Joyce’s private schemas and later editorial practice; the 1922 edition did not print the Homeric episode titles. ## Story and technique

[T] At about eight in the morning Stephen Dedalus shares the Martello tower with Buck Mulligan and the English visitor Haines. Mulligan stages a blasphemous mock Mass with a shaving bowl, needles Stephen about his dead mother, and treats the tower as his social property. Haines asks Stephen about Irish art and belief; Stephen answers through fragments of theology, colonial history, and private grief. The three walk toward the school and shore. At the bathing cove Stephen hands Mulligan the tower key and two pennies for a pint, then leaves his companions and ends the episode thinking of himself as dispossessed.

[T] The technique is already unstable and layered: external action is brief, while Stephen’s inward associations turn ordinary objects into theological and political signs. Mulligan’s jokes, Haines’s anthropological curiosity, and Stephen’s compressed interior monologue compete for authority. [I] The opening therefore makes interpretation itself a contested household: whoever controls the ritual, language, and key claims the right to define the place.

## Important passages and close reading

[T] The opening parody of the Latin Mass turns the razor, mirror, and shaving bowl into props that echo a chalice, cross, and priestly rite. [I] It is not simply secular mockery: Mulligan borrows sacred form because ritual gives his performance social force. Stephen’s silence makes his exclusion visible; he can reject the rite yet remains wounded by its misuse.

[T] The sea is praised as a “great sweet mother” and then rendered “snotgreen” and “scrotumtightening.” [I] Maternal, bodily, national, and Homeric associations overlap without settling into one symbol. The sea offers an epic horizon, but Stephen experiences it through disgust and memory of his dying mother.

[T] Stephen calls himself a servant of an English and an Italian master, then imagines a third master who wants him for odd jobs. [I] The line joins colonial rule, the church, and economic dependence without reducing them to a single allegory. The “usurper” ending leaves the central question unresolved: whether Mulligan has stolen a home, a language, or Stephen’s confidence in his own inheritance.

## Homeric echo and crucial difference

[C] Joyce’s “Telemachus” echoes the opening movement of the *Odyssey*, where Telemachus lives in a compromised household while suitors consume his property and Athena prompts him toward action. Mulligan and Haines resemble competing forms of intrusion, and Stephen’s absent father and contested dwelling invite the comparison.

[I] The difference is decisive: Stephen is an adult artist, not a young prince preparing a search for Odysseus; there is no Penelope-centered household and no departure to find a living father. The echo supplies pressure around inheritance and authority, not a one-to-one plot substitution.

## Reading question

How does the mock Mass make the tower feel both like a home and like a stage on which Stephen’s authority has already been taken?
