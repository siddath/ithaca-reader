# Episode 6 — “Hades”

**Scope.** “Hades” is a title from Joyce’s private schemas, retained here as a research label; interpretive comparisons remain provisional. Locators refer to `sources/ulysses-joyce.txt` by searchable phrase and full-text source line number.

## Story and technique

[T] Bloom rides with Martin Cunningham, Simon Dedalus, and Mr Power to Paddy Dignam’s funeral. The carriage passes Dublin streets, then the men walk through Glasnevin cemetery. Bloom thinks about burial, cremation, ghosts, the dead, and his own family losses while the other men move between sympathy, gossip, and social embarrassment. Hynes asks Bloom’s name for the newspaper account; Bloom answers, “L, Mr Bloom said. Leopold,” and adds M’Coy’s name.

[T] The episode’s movement is public and collective, but its consciousness remains intimate. [I] Bloom’s attention to the material work of death—washing, coffins, soil, decay—prevents the funeral from becoming a purely elevated rite. The dead are ordinary, and that is precisely why they matter.

## Important passages and close reading

[T] Bloom imagines the corpse as “meat gone bad,” compares burial with cremation and exposure, and wonders how the dead continue to occupy the living imagination. [I] The material language is not nihilistic alone. By refusing to idealize the body, Bloom keeps grief attached to care, labor, and memory.

[T] The “brown macintosh” figure and the count of mourners disturb the group’s social order. Bloom notices who belongs, who is absent, and who may be remembered incorrectly. [I] The cemetery becomes a democratic archive, though one still shaped by class, religion, and the newspaper’s need to name people.

## Homeric echo and crucial difference

[C] “Hades” recalls Odysseus’ descent among the dead in *Odyssey* Book 11. Bloom’s procession is a civic, secularized underworld: graves, inscriptions, and remembered Dubliners replace heroic shades and prophecy. [I] No oracle tells Bloom what to do. The knowledge gained is modest but ethical—the living must keep making room for the dead without pretending death is sublime.

[I] Bloom’s Jewish identity and outsider status also complicate the Christian funeral setting. He participates through sympathy and observation rather than doctrinal belonging, making the episode’s “underworld” a shared city whose boundaries remain visible.

## Reading question

How does Bloom’s insistence on the physical facts of burial change what a public funeral can mean?
