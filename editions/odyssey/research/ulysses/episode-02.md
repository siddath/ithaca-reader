# Episode 2 — “Nestor”

**Scope.** “Nestor” is a title from Joyce’s private schemas, retained here as a research label; the 1922 edition did not print the Homeric episode titles. This dossier uses conventional episode numbers and schema-derived titles. Interpretive comparisons remain provisional. Locators refer to `sources/ulysses-joyce.txt` by searchable phrase and full-text source line number.

## Story and technique

[T] Stephen teaches a history lesson on Pyrrhus at the boys’ school, where the pupils’ jokes expose the distance between textbook knowledge and lived experience. After class, the headmaster Garrett Deasy gives Stephen a letter about foot-and-mouth disease and asks him to place it with the press. Deasy then launches into a racist account of English national decline and Jewish financial power. Stephen leaves with the letter, the boys’ shouts behind him, and the sea and history pressing on his thoughts.

[T] The episode moves between classroom dialogue, remembered fragments, and Stephen’s silent commentary. [I] Its “Nestor” resemblance is therefore less a claim that Deasy is wise than a test of how authority presents itself: an old man’s aphorisms and patriotic confidence are set beside childish recitation and Stephen’s inability to find a usable history.

## Important passages and close reading

[T] The boys’ lesson makes the past a memorized sequence of names and dates, while Stephen’s associations turn Pyrrhus’s “victory” into a pattern of costly success. When the boys cannot solve Stephen’s riddle, he supplies the fox burying its grandmother under a hollybush. [I] The answer is comic and opaque: it sounds like a proverb whose meaning has survived after its explanatory story has disappeared. History is both buried knowledge and an active force that keeps returning.

[T] Deasy’s letter translates a public-health issue into nationalist economics. His rhetoric then shifts from cattle to conspiracy and “national decay.” [I] Joyce places the material fact of disease beside the fantasy of racial contamination, allowing the reader to see how authority can convert uncertainty into blame. The scene’s moral pressure comes from the contrast between Deasy’s confidence and Stephen’s refusal to accept a coherent historical goal.

[C] The “Nestor” tag recalls Telemachus’s visit to Nestor in *Odyssey* Book 3, where an older king offers memories and hospitality. [I] Deasy offers neither reliable wisdom nor generous hospitality. His office becomes a colonial classroom in which “history” serves present power.

## Homeric echo and crucial difference

[C] The episode echoes the Nestor encounter through an older male authority, a sonlike visitor, and a conversation about the past. [I] Its difference is central: Deasy’s history is self-serving and exclusionary, while Homer’s Nestor is a host and witness whose memories help the younger man act. Stephen leaves with a piece of propaganda rather than a practical genealogy.

## Reading question

When Stephen calls history a nightmare, is he rejecting the past, or asking what kind of account could let the living act without repeating it?
