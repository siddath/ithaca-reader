# Joyce’s *Ulysses* and Homer’s *Odyssey*

**Scope.** This dossier provisionally treats “*Ulysses*” as James Joyce’s 1922 novel. The film mentioned in the larger project has no title, year, or cut specified, so this file makes no film claims. “Ulysses” is the Latin form of the Greek name Odysseus; the shared title signals a deliberate relation, not an identity between the two protagonists.

[C] Homer’s poem has twenty-four books and follows a long, nonlinear return from the Trojan War, including Telemachus’ search, Odysseus’ wanderings, the Ithacan recognition, and the contest with the suitors. Joyce’s novel has eighteen episodes, conventionally numbered for reference, and compresses its action into Dublin from the morning of 16 June 1904 through the night and early hours of 17 June. The numbers do not map: one Homeric book may inform several episodes, and one Joyce episode may gather material from several Homeric scenes. Joyce’s private schema titles are established labels used here as research headings; the interpretations and correspondences remain provisional.

| [5. “Lotus Eaters”](research/ulysses/episode-05.md) | Book 9: the lotus that makes sailors forget home | [M] Baths, flowers, advertising, church ritual, and urban drift offer distributed forms of postponement. | [6. “Hades”](research/ulysses/episode-06.md) | Book 11: Odysseus among the dead | [M] Dignam’s funeral makes a civic, material underworld of Glasnevin. [I] Bloom receives no oracle; the knowledge is the modest obligation to remember ordinary dead people. |
| [7. “Aeolus”](research/ulysses/episode-07.md) | Book 10: Aeolus and the winds | [M] The newspaper office becomes a machine of editorial gusts and public phrases. [I] Rhetorical dispersal replaces a literal storm or wind bag. |
| [8. “Lestrygonians”](research/ulysses/episode-08.md) | Book 10: the cannibal giants who destroy Odysseus’ fleet | [M] Eating, crowding, and bodily threat make Dublin’s social arrangements look predatory. [I] Bloom faces disgust and exclusion, not a supernatural fleet attack. |
| [9. “Scylla and Charybdis”](research/ulysses/episode-09.md) | Book 12: the passage between two sea dangers | [M] The library debate places material biography and metaphysical speculation under pressure. | [10. “Wandering Rocks”](research/ulysses/episode-10.md) | [C] Book 12: Circe describes the Wandering Rocks as an alternative route; the Argo passed them, but Odysseus does not sail them | [M] Dublin’s intersecting routes replace a ship’s navigation. [I] The indirect mapping matters: the form resists reducing a distributed city to a disguised voyage. |
| [11. “Sirens”](research/ulysses/episode-11.md) | Book 12: the Sirens’ song | [C] Homer’s song promises dangerous knowledge of Troy and the wider world. [I] Joyce’s music carries grief, memory, and social seduction; [A] a purely erotic reading misses the episode’s epistemic lure. |
| [12. “Cyclops”](research/ulysses/episode-12.md) | Book 9: Polyphemus and the cave encounter | [M] The Citizen’s pub is a social enclosure, and one-eyed nationalism narrows belonging. [I] Bloom is not a Greek raider using a planned trick; the “monster” is repeatable public speech. |
| [13. “Nausicaa”](research/ulysses/episode-13.md) | Book 6: Nausicaa meets the shipwrecked Odysseus, gives directions and aid, and does not escort him into court | [M] Beach, young woman, stranger, and imagined social renewal invite the echo. [I] Joyce reverses rescue: erotic distance and print romance replace practical guidance toward a civic welcome. |
| [14. “Oxen of the Sun”](research/ulysses/episode-14.md) | Book 12: the cattle of Helios | [M] Appetite, fertility, and consequence move into a maternity hospital and a history of English styles. | [15. “Circe”](research/ulysses/episode-15.md) | Book 10: Circe’s transformations and later hospitality | [M] Bella/Bello is the clearest local transformation analogue, but no single sorceress controls the episode. | [16. “Eumaeus”](research/ulysses/episode-16.md) | Books 14–16: Eumaeus’ shelter and the return’s recognition work | [M] The cabman’s shelter is a modern refuge, and Murphy supplies a wandering counterpart. [I] Bloom’s hospitality is generous but awkward; Stephen’s acceptance does not become recognition or adoption. |
| [17. “Ithaca”](research/ulysses/episode-17.md) | Books 13–23: return, household, recognition, and home | [M] Bloom reaches 7 Eccles Street and Stephen occupies a possible son-position. [I] No secret identity, bow contest, or purge produces a restored household; companionship remains temporary and knowledge incomplete. |
| [18. “Penelope”](research/ulysses/episode-18.md) | Book 23: Penelope, recognition, and the return to the marital bed | [M] Molly’s domestic monologue closes the novel in a space associated with Penelope. [I] She is not a copy of Homeric chastity or strategy: her voice includes desire, resentment, work, infidelity, and affirmation. |

This map is a set of pressure points, not a decoding key. [A] “Wandering Rocks” draws on a route described in Book 12 but not traversed by Odysseus; Joyce can develop an offered possibility rather than reproduce the hero’s actual itinerary. ## The major differences

### Heroism

[C] Homer’s Odysseus is a warrior, strategist, speaker, and survivor whose return is supported and obstructed by gods. [T] Bloom is a Jewish advertising canvasser moving through errands, meals, a funeral, a pub, a maternity hospital, Nighttown, and home; Stephen is a young intellectual whose resistance often takes the form of withdrawal or verbal performance. [I] Joyce relocates heroic scale into endurance, attention, improvisation, and care. That does not make Bloom a disguised Odysseus or declare ordinary life morally superior to epic action. It changes what a narrative is willing to treat as consequential.

### Revenge and violence

[C] The *Odyssey* culminates in Odysseus’ violent defeat of the suitors and the reassertion of household authority. [T] Joyce gives no equivalent purge. Bloom is insulted by nationalists, exposed to erotic betrayal, and caught in Nighttown violence; he responds through argument, evasion, practical protection, or departure. [I] The absence of revenge is structurally meaningful, but it is not a modern forgiveness lesson. Bloom’s restraint coexists with jealousy, prejudice, erotic fantasy, and the possibility that leaving is simply what the scene permits.

### Home and recognition

[C] Ithaca is both destination and test: the household must recognize the returning man, and Odysseus must recognize the household’s signs. [T] Bloom returns to 7 Eccles Street, but Molly’s affair has already changed the marital home; Stephen enters as a possible son-like companion and leaves before adoption or permanent friendship. [I] Joyce keeps the desire for home while removing the epic guarantee that home can restore identity. Domestic objects and routines offer continuity; they do not prove that the people sharing them understand one another.

### Sexuality and the body

[C] Homer’s poem includes sexual relations with Calypso and Circe, marriage, courtship, fertility, and the suitors’ pressure on Penelope, but these are organized through the epic’s social and divine structures. [T] Joyce places masturbation, adultery, pornography, appetite, menstruation, pregnancy, childbirth, and bodily waste inside the reader’s field. Episodes 4, 5, 8, 13, 14, 15, and 18 make desire materially difficult to separate from shame, commerce, care, and power. [I] The body is neither a universal key nor a simple “real” beneath language. Joyce keeps asking how bodies are described, fantasized, regulated, and misread.

### Religion and ritual

[C] Olympian gods intervene directly in Homer’s plot, while sacrifice and divine favor organize the poem’s moral weather. [T] Joyce’s Dublin contains Catholic ritual, Protestant and Jewish identities, blasphemy, Christian vocabulary, Jewish memory, and colonial institutions. Mulligan’s mock Mass, the church in “Lotus Eaters,” Dignam’s funeral, the maternity hospital’s quasi-sacred language, and Bloom’s outsider observations all use ritual as social form. [I] Joyce does not replace gods with one secular doctrine. Ritual continues to shape belonging and exclusion even when belief is uncertain or ironic.

### Narration and form

[C] The *Odyssey* works through formulaic oral verse, repeated epithets, embedded tales, direct speeches, divine interventions, and shifts between present action and remembered journeys. [T] Joyce changes method from episode to episode: free indirect discourse, interior monologue, newspaper headlines, multiple city cuts, musical recurrence, parody of prose history, a stage script, exhausted pseudo-journalism, catechism, and unpunctuated monologue. [I] The formal variety is not a decorative catalogue of techniques. It changes who can know an event and what counts as evidence. “Ithaca” promises exhaustive answers while showing that intimacy exceeds measurement; “Penelope” gives extended speech while leaving interpretation open.

### History, empire, and the present

[C] Homer’s return is shaped by the aftermath of Troy and by mythic genealogies that turn a household crisis into a story about order. [T] Joyce’s date is an ordinary day under British rule: newspapers, advertising, the viceroy’s procession, nationalist rhetoric, antisemitism, Irish memory, and the language of empire surround the characters. Stephen’s “nightmare” formulation and Bloom’s civic definition of a nation are arguments made inside this historical pressure, not timeless philosophical theses. [I] The Homeric frame gives Joyce a way to ask what “return” means when the city is already structured by other people’s histories. The frame does not erase 1904 Dublin or convert colonial experience into a decorative myth.
