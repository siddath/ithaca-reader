# Before the first book: the world of the Odyssey

This is the mythology and conceptual framework needed to read the whole poem, with routes into the wider Greek tradition. It is not an exhaustive encyclopedia of every Greek myth. Ancient traditions disagree; we identify which text supplies a detail rather than manufacture one definitive family tree. Labels follow [the research contract](CONTRACT.md).

## Three different things called Homer, Odyssey and Ulysses

**[C] Homer** is the traditional author-name of the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*. Their emergence from oral poetic traditions is central to modern scholarship; a precise biography and a single, securely dated act of composition are not available. Repeated epithets, type-scenes and recurring phrases belong to the poems' performance techniques, not evidence that the poet forgot he had said something. The relation of oral composition to fixation in writing remains debated. See [Nagy on oral traditions, texts and authorship](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/curated-article/gregory-nagy-oral-traditions-written-texts-and-questions-of-authorship/), a particular scholarly account rather than a final settlement.

**[T/C] The Odyssey** is an ancient Greek epic divided in its transmitted form into 24 books. “Book” here is the usual term for what we might call a large chapter. **Odysseus, Ulysses and Ulixes** name the same mythological man in Greek and Latin-derived usage. Butler calls him Ulysses. That does not mean Butler is translating Joyce.

**[T/C] James Joyce's Ulysses**, published in 1922, is a separate novel in 18 episodes. Its Dublin narrative runs through 16 June 1904 and into the following early morning. The familiar Homeric episode names are supplied by Joyce's schemas and critical usage; they were not printed as chapter headings in the original novel. The provisional companion here follows the [pre-1923 text supplied by Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/4300), not a silently mixed modern edition. See [the Joyce Project's schema discussion](https://joyceproject.com/notes/zU-bJZUBeQi-aLQfd66b).

## The story's order and the events' order [T]

Read in the order Homer gives us. The delay in meeting Odysseus makes us experience the space he has left in other people's lives. His later first-person narrative then lets us ask how a man recounts his own responsibility to listeners whose help he needs.

1. Books 1–4: Telemachus and the absent father.
2. Books 5–8: Odysseus leaves Calypso and reaches Phaeacia.
3. Books 9–12: Odysseus tells the earlier wanderings in retrospect.
4. Books 13–16: Return to Ithaca, disguise, and reunion with Telemachus.
5. Books 17–20: The beggar in his own house.
6. Books 21–24: The bow, vengeance, recognition, and imposed peace.

In event order, the adventure sequence is: Troy → Cicones → Lotus-eaters → Cyclops → Aeolus → Laestrygonians → Circe → encounter with the dead → Circe again → Sirens → Scylla and Charybdis → cattle of Helios → shipwreck and Charybdis again → Calypso → Phaeacia → Ithaca. Books 9–12 tell the middle of this sequence retrospectively; Books 1–4 follow events elsewhere near the end of Odysseus's absence. The traditional broad frame is ten years of war and ten years before return, not twenty years at sea. Much of the second period is prolonged detention or residence, not continuous sailing.

The poem is not a reliable modern navigation chart. It names some recognisable places and some marvellous ones. Proposed identifications of Circe's island, the Lotus-eaters' country or the wandering rocks are hypotheses; we do not draw a falsely precise route through modern borders.

## The Trojan background [T/C]

The *Iliad* focuses on a portion of the war, particularly Achilles' anger; it ends with Hector's funeral. It does not narrate the wooden horse and Troy's fall. The *Odyssey* recalls the horse in Books 4 and 8 and repeatedly considers what happened to the victors afterwards. Other ancient poems, summaries and tragedies supply further episodes. Their existence does not make every later elaboration part of Homer's narration.

| Household or relationship | Essential members | Why it matters here |
|---|---|---|
| Ithaca | Laertes and Anticleia; their son Odysseus; Penelope; Telemachus | Father, husband and son must be recognised in different ways. Eurycleia, Eumaeus and Philoetius are essential to the household but do not have the family's freedom or status. |
| Sparta | Menelaus, Helen, Hermione; Megapenthes is Menelaus's son by an enslaved woman | A returned couple tells divergent stories about the war. Wealth and reunion do not erase loss. |
| Agamemnon's house | Agamemnon, Clytemnestra, Aegisthus, Orestes | The repeated warning: the returning victor may be killed at home. Penelope must not be flattened into the opposite of a caricatured Clytemnestra. |
| Achilles' family | Peleus, Thetis, Achilles, Neoptolemus | The dead hero's concern for his son and his preference for life trouble a simple cult of glorious death. |
| Phaeacia | Alcinous, Arete, Nausicaa; Poseidon in the genealogy | A community whose hospitality makes the return possible and exposes it to divine retaliation. |

The standard wider Trojan narrative includes the judgement of Paris and Helen's departure with Paris. Accounts differ about coercion, choice and divine agency. Homeric Helen's own explanations and other characters' responses deserve to be read before a single moral label is applied to her.

## Divine generations: a wider framework [C]

In [Hesiod's Theogony](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodTheogony.html), translated by H. G. Evelyn-White (1914), the ordering of the divine world develops through generations and struggles. Chaos, Earth/Gaia, Tartarus and Eros belong near the beginning; Gaia brings forth Sky/Uranus. Gaia and Uranus produce the Titans, including Cronus and Rhea. Cronus displaces Uranus, swallows his own children to prevent a successor, and is overcome by Zeus, whom Rhea saves through concealment and substitution. Zeus and his allies defeat the Titans and establish their order. Consult the poem's sections beginning at verses 116, 453, 617 and 881.

This genealogy explains the recurring Homeric description of Zeus as son of Cronus, but the *Odyssey* does not retell the whole succession myth. The older order is not simply “evil Titans” versus “good Olympians.” Divinity includes kinship, rivalry, violence, obligation and negotiated authority.

Hesiod also relates Zeus's swallowing of Metis and Athena's birth (886 onward), connecting rule and intelligence. **[I]** That background can enrich a reading of Athena's strategic affinity with Odysseus. It does not prove that Athena is only an abstract personification of his intelligence.

**Important variant:** Aphrodite's birth from the sea after Uranus's mutilation is Hesiodic; the adultery song in *Odyssey* 8 calls her Zeus's daughter. Do not “correct” one text by quietly replacing it with the other. Likewise Hesiod's divine craftsmen called Cyclopes should not automatically be treated as identical in every feature to Polyphemus's pastoral community.

## Gods and powers we actually need

The [Metropolitan Museum's account of Greek religion](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/greek-gods-and-religious-practices) describes a plurality of gods and practices without one exclusive scriptural authority. Offerings, sacrifice and local sanctuaries matter alongside stories. The following roles are grounded chiefly in the *Odyssey* passages named, rather than in a universal list of divine “jobs.”

| Greek name | Butler's usual name | Action in the poem and a reading caution |
|---|---|---|
| Zeus | Jove/Jupiter | Councils, warnings, hospitality and the final restraint on violence (1, 5, 12, 24). His justice is voiced and enacted in particular cases; avoid assuming a consistent modern theodicy. |
| Athena | Minerva | Mentor, protector, strategist, disguiser; also supports lethal restoration (1–3, 5–8, 13–24). Wisdom here is not equivalent to pacifism or universal impartiality. |
| Poseidon | Neptune | Father of Polyphemus, persecutor of Odysseus, ancestor of Phaeacians (1, 5, 9, 13). Familial and divine claims conflict with other obligations. |
| Hermes | Mercury | Messenger to Calypso, helper against Circe, conductor of the suitors' souls (5, 10, 24). Movement across boundaries is a recurring function. |
| Helios | Sun/Hyperion in Butler's usage | Cattle protected by a warning; demands redress (12). Do not silently substitute Apollo for the Sun in this episode. |
| Apollo | Apollo | Song, prophecy, lethal arrows; invoked around the bow (8, 21). Later identifications with the Sun do not erase distinctions within this poem. |
| Artemis | Diana | Comparison for Nausicaa; death and female life appear in her associations (6, 15, 20). A simile does not make a mortal woman a goddess. |
| Aphrodite | Venus | Desire and divine adultery in the embedded song (8); invoked in Helen's explanation (4). The song is a representation inside a representation. |
| Ares; Hephaestus | Mars; Vulcan | Lover and husband in the net story (8); craft defeats physical prowess. Their roles in that song are not the whole of Greek theology. |
| Hades; Persephone | Hades; Proserpine | The dead and their realm, especially 10–11. Hades may name the god or his domain. It is not simply Christian hell. |
| Calypso | Calypso | Immortal captor and eventual helper, daughter of Atlas (1, 5, 7). Her complaint about inequality coexists with her coercion of Odysseus. |
| Circe | Circe | Daughter of Helios; transforms the crew, then hosts and guides them (10–12). Her role changes; “temptress” alone is inadequate. |
| Ino-Leucothea | Ino/Leucothea | A formerly mortal sea goddess whose veil helps the shipwrecked hero (5). Do not import every later account of her life without attribution. |
| Proteus; Eidothea | Proteus; Idothea | A resistant source of knowledge and the daughter who supplies a method for capturing him (4). |
| Muse(s) | Muse(s) | Authorise narration and the bard's song (1, 8). Inspiration and the human situation of performance interact. |
| Erinyes; Themis; river gods; nymphs | Names vary | Kinship obligations, assembly order and local divine powers matter (2, 5, 13). The divine world extends beyond the familiar Olympian group. |

## Monsters and boundaries [T/I]

| Encounter | What the passage gives us | Productive interpretive question |
|---|---|---|
| Lotus-eaters, 9 | A food that removes the wish to return | What happens to a person's commitments when present pleasure makes them irrelevant? This is an analogy about desire, not a clinical addiction diagnosis. |
| Polyphemus, 9 | Pastoral order, physical power, cannibalism, refused hospitality, a name trick | What distinguishes strength, intelligence and ethical conduct? Odysseus's trespass and insistence on gifts remain part of the scene. |
| Aeolus, 10 | Contained winds, a gift misunderstood and opened | How can unequal information and distrust defeat competent leadership? Aeolus is appointed keeper of winds here; later genealogies need separate treatment. |
| Laestrygonians, 10 | A harbour that becomes a trap, most ships destroyed | Can apparent shelter expose a group to concentrated danger? The one ship kept outside becomes decisive. |
| Circe's transformations, 10 | Human awareness inside altered animal bodies | Which features constitute humanity: shape, memory, behaviour, recognition? The men are not said simply to deserve transformation for gluttony. |
| The dead, 11 | Burial claims, prophecy, family grief, famous shades, punishments | Which accomplishments retain value when bodily life is lost? |
| Sirens, 12 | Enchanting song promising knowledge; bones around them | Can the desire to know become incompatible with continuing to live and act? The text does not describe them as mermaids. |
| Scylla and Charybdis, 12 | A six-headed predator and a consuming whirlpool; a forced perilous passage | How does one decide when every available course risks loss, and what should followers be told? |
| Cattle of Helios, 12 | An explicit prohibition broken under hunger and delay | How do responsibility, necessity and leadership interact when survival pressure grows? |

The monsters are beings in the narrated world before they become interpretive figures. A universal key such as “every island is a stage of consciousness” can be explored as later reception, but it is not established by the primary text.

## The dead and the larger mythic network [T/C]

Book 11 includes Elpenor's unburied shade, Tiresias, Anticleia, a catalogue of women, Agamemnon, Achilles, Ajax, and figures such as Minos, Orion, Tityus, Tantalus, Sisyphus and Heracles. The individual dossier explains the scene's movement; here are the boundaries worth remembering.

Elpenor gives burial a concrete social urgency. Anticleia tells Odysseus what his absence has done at home; three failed embraces expose the separation between bodily life and shades. Achilles' preference for low-status living over rule among the dead prevents “immortal fame” from becoming an uncomplicated reward. Ajax's silence preserves an unresolved quarrel. Tityus's torment, Tantalus's inaccessible food and water, and Sisyphus's returning stone are particular scenes, not proof that all shades undergo moral punishment. Heracles appears with a striking distinction between his image below and his place with the gods above; treat the transmitted text's complication openly rather than smoothing it into one universal afterlife scheme.

The catalogue of women includes Tyro, Antiope, Alcmena, Epicaste and others whose families connect this epic to wider heroic traditions. Epicaste is the name here for the mother-wife associated with Oedipus; Sophocles' later dramatic version is not the entire source of this tradition. Circe and Aeetes connect through Helios to the Argonautic world; Book 12 recalls the Argo. [Hesiod's Theogony 956 onward](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodTheogony.html) supplies additional genealogical links to Medea and Jason. These links help orient us; they do not make the *Odyssey* a continuous retelling of all their adventures.

## Philosophical vocabulary for a close reading

These are working glosses. Meaning changes by passage and translation; they are not one-word equations.

| Term | Working sense | Where to test it |
|---|---|---|
| *Nostos* | Return/homecoming, also its song in some scholarly readings | 1, 5, 13, 23–24: physical arrival versus renewed belonging |
| *Kleos* | What is heard about a person; renown carried through story | 1, 8–9, 11, 24: fame can preserve a name and endanger its bearer |
| *Mētis* | Resourceful, strategic intelligence | 9, 13, 19, 21–23: a disguise can protect, manipulate or recognise |
| *Oikos* | Household as people, property and continuing estate | 1–2, 14, 17–24: affection and ownership coexist |
| *Xenia* | Guest-host relations and their obligations | 1, 3–4, 6–9, 14, 17: aid, reciprocity, limits and abuse |
| *Aidos* | Respect, shame, regard for others; context-sensitive | 6, 8, 17: a feeling can restrain cruelty or protect rank |
| *Sēma* | A sign or marker | 19, 23–24: scar, bed and trees support different recognitions |
| *Homophrosynē* | Concord/shared-mindedness | 6 and 23: a marriage includes shared intelligence, not merely reunion |
| *Hybris* | Outrageous or overbearing conduct | Suitor behaviour: do not reduce every instance to private “pride” |

Use [Nagy's passage commentary](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/curated-article/gregory-nagy-a-sampling-of-comments-on-the-iliad-and-odyssey/) for philological follow-up. The philosophical questions in these dossiers are our explicit interpretations of scenes. Stoicism, Plato, existentialism, psychoanalysis and modern trauma theory may offer later comparisons, but Homer is not being assigned membership in those schools.

## What to mark while reading

Mark **who speaks**, **who knows**, **who works**, **who may refuse**, and **who pays the cost**. Add a second mark whenever a scene repeats with a changed outcome: feeding a stranger, weaving, weeping at a song, testing a report, recognising a body, returning home. These repeated acts make the poem's reasoning visible without requiring a secret symbolic code.

The most consequential sequence for slow reading is: the opening debate over blame (1); Penelope's shroud (2); Helen's paired stories (4); the mortal home versus immortality (5); Nausicaa's reception (6); the captive-woman simile (8); Nobody and the shouted name (9); Achilles and Anticleia (11); Sirens and the cattle (12); the scar and Penelope's dream (19); the executions (22); the rooted bed (23); the peace imposed after further killing (24). The other books supply the relationships and repetitions that prevent these highlights from becoming isolated slogans.
